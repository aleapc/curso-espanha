The table, written down

Coffee or a caña standing at the counter: nothing, or the loose coins out of your change. Neighbourhood bar, or a menú del día: round the total up — fifty cents, a euro. A mid-range restaurant where the evening was genuinely lovely: five to ten per cent, and it is worth knowing that most people at the tables around you leave less than that, or nothing at all. Somewhere smart: up to ten per cent, after a glance at whether 'servicio' is already on the bill. Taxi: round it up. The porter who carries your cases, or the person who cleans your room: a euro or two, in cash. One honest caveat about all of the above, and it is the reason there are no averages in this part: there is no published series for what people actually leave in Spain — no figure from the national statistics office and none from the hospitality federation. What you have here is the convergent advice of field guides, which is why it is a shape rather than a number.

Está bien así, gracias. — ehs-TAH byehn ah-SEE — that's fine as it is, thanks. The four words, and they fit every counter
Quédese con el cambio. — KEH-dheh-seh kon el KAHM-byoh — keep the change, for the porter or the driver who got out of the car

Why it all stays so small

The structural fact underneath the whole table is that hospitality staff in Spain are on a salary and a collective agreement. The tip is a bonus on top, not the income, and that single sentence does more work than any amount of etiquette: it means leaving nothing harms nobody and is not read as meanness. It also explains why over-tipping does not buy what an American hand expects it to buy. Nobody was waiting for it, so twenty per cent cannot read as generous — it simply reads as somebody who has not worked out where they are. If you want the arithmetic, here it is with the working shown: an average menú del día is €14.20 (Hostelería de España and Edenred, 2026). A twenty per cent reflex on that is €2.84; rounding to €15 is €0.80; the gap is about two euros a meal. Two paid meals a day for a fortnight is twenty-eight meals, so roughly €57 a head, about €115 for a couple. The British ten per cent reflex costs around €17 a head over the same trip. Treat all of that as a projection off one published average, not as a measurement — and then forget it, because the money was never the interesting part.

Son catorce con veinte. — sohn kah-TOR-theh kon BAYN-teh — 👂 that's fourteen twenty. Two 'th' sounds and the price of lunch

Todo junto, por separado, a partes iguales — and the round that comes back to you

Three ways a bill gets settled, and the waiter will usually ask you which before he rings anything up. 'Todo junto' is all on one — you are getting this. 'Pagamos por separado' is each of us our own, and the important half of that one, from part fourteen, is that you say it before he closes anything rather than after. 'Pagamos a partes iguales' is one total divided evenly, which is the easiest of the three for him and the commonest at a table of friends. Splitting a bill item by item is friction here and generally does not happen. The other custom sitting alongside it is the round: one person settles the whole thing and the next one comes round to you. 'Invito yo' is what you will hear, said quickly and said as a fact; the honest answer is not to get your wallet out but 'la próxima invito yo', and then actually to do it the next morning. The wrestling match over the little plate is the part that turns money into a scene, and losing that wrestling match gracefully is the skill. Two places where it does not apply: somebody you have just met and will not see again, and a bill big enough to hurt — there, 'por separado', early and cheerfully.

¿Va todo junto o por separado? — bah TOH-dhoh KHOON-toh — 👂 all on one, or separately? The question that arrives before the bill does
La próxima invito yo. — lah PROHK-see-mah een-BEE-toh yoh — the next one's on me. It settles this one and books the next

Who you are talking to, and why 'está bien así' dodges the question

'Está bien así, gracias' has a quiet virtue that is easy to miss: it contains nothing at all about the person opposite you. No tú, no usted, no decision to make while somebody is holding a card machine. That is exactly why it is the workhorse of this part and why it turns up at a bar counter, a hotel doorway and a station café without changing a syllable. 'Quédese con el cambio' does carry that decision — it is the usted shape — and we recorded it that way because the two places you are most likely to want it are an older man carrying your cases and a driver who has got out of the car to lift the boot. In a young bar, among people already on tú with you, 'quédate con el cambio' is the version flying about, and nothing breaks if you use the one you remember. If you want to sidestep the whole question, hand the money over and say the four words instead. That is what most of this part is quietly built to let you do.

Quédate con el cambio. — KEH-dhah-teh kon el KAHM-byoh — 👂 the tú version, for a bar where you are already on first-name terms

The free tour, and the ten minutes afterwards

The walking tour advertised as free is the single role you will meet in Spain with a real expectation attached to it, and it works differently from everything else in this part: what people put into the guide's hand at the end is not a bonus on top of a wage, it is the wage. The guide has usually paid a per-head fee to the company that sent you, so a group that drifts off costs her money rather than merely disappointing her. Practicalities, in order. Get change before the walk ends — 'quería cambio, por favor' at any counter, because nobody hands over a fifty and nobody has a card machine in a square. Go up to her rather than joining the vague semicircle: a note in the hand, eye contact, 'muchas gracias, de verdad'. And then stay for the ten minutes almost nobody waits for, which is when you ask where to eat tonight and get an answer from a person who walks that street every day. That answer is routinely worth more than the walk was. A ticketed tour you booked and paid for online is a different animal entirely: it was settled at the checkout and nothing further is expected of you.
