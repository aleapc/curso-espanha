August, and the card taped to the shutter

The thing to understand about August is that it is not a slow month, it is an absent one, and only for a particular kind of business: the shop with one family in it. They shut, they put a handwritten card on the glass — CERRADO POR VACACIONES — and they come back in September. Nothing about this is a sign of trouble, and the neighbours all know exactly who is away and where they have gone. What makes it cost you something is not the closure but the listing: opening hours online are very often the ones the shop keeps in March, and a month-long holiday rarely gets typed in anywhere. Hence the rule that is worth more than any sentence in this part — in August, ring before you walk. What does not shut, and this is the half that never gets said: the department stores, the big supermarket chains, the restaurants, the bars, the museums, the galleries and anything on a street that lives off visitors. Sitting inside the month is the fifteenth, la Asunción, a public holiday across the whole country. Plan a supermarket run for the fourteenth and the fifteenth stops mattering. And if you are in the south or the interior, the middle of an August afternoon is not walking time regardless of what anybody is open: two in the afternoon in Seville in August is a taxi, not a stroll, and the shuttered street at four is partly the reason the evening starts at nine.

CERRADO POR VACACIONES — theh-RRAH-dhoh por bah-kah-THYOH-nehs — 👂 closed for the holidays. It means the month, not the afternoon
¿Están abiertos hoy? — ehs-TAHN ah-BYEHR-tohs oi — are you open today? The four words that replace the twenty-five minute walk

Two ranges on the card, not one

A small shop's day has a hole in the middle of it. Roughly: open around ten, down at half one or two, back up at half four or five, and then trading until eight or half eight — which is why the horario card by the door has two ranges printed on it and why reading only the first one is how people end up outside a locked chemist at three. This is commercial, not biological: almost nobody is asleep. The country is at lunch, which is the big meal, and lunch takes a proper hour and a half. What ignores the hole entirely: El Corte Inglés and the other department stores, Mercadona, Lidl, Carrefour, Zara and the chains generally, every restaurant (it is their peak), and the museums. So the practical shape of an afternoon is that the errand goes before half one or after five, and the middle belongs to eating and to anything with a roof and air conditioning. One more card worth knowing by sight: HORARIO DE VERANO, summer opening hours, which is a different and usually shorter timetable taped over the normal one. If two cards are on the glass, the seasonal one is the one that counts.

¿A qué hora abren por la tarde? — ah keh OH-rah AH-brehn por lah TAR-dheh — what time do you open in the afternoon?
HORARIO DE VERANO — oh-RAH-ryoh deh beh-RAH-noh — 👂 summer hours. A second card over the first one

Fourteen days, and the two that belong to one town

Spanish labour law caps paid public holidays at fourteen a year, and it says explicitly that two of them are local. That single sentence explains almost everything odd you will meet in a calendar here. Nine or so are national, a few more are set by the region, and then the town hall of the place you happen to be standing in picks two — nearly always the patron saint of the town or of the neighbourhood. The consequence for a visitor is the one nobody warns you about: a Wednesday can be completely shut in one town and a normal working day in the town twenty minutes down the road, and no national calendar you look up will show it. What actually closes is the official layer — banks, town halls, post offices, many chemists, most small shops, some museums. What does not close is hospitality: a local holiday is one of the best trading days of the year for the bars and the restaurants, and they open early for it. The move is trivial and it is the whole point of this part: ask at the hotel desk, on arrival, which days that week are local ones, and put the bank and the paperwork on either side of them. Holy Week works on the same logic with a moving date, since it is tied to Easter and shifts every year — the useful thing to know about it is that in a lot of towns the calendar is less disrupted than the street map is.

¿Mañana es fiesta? — mah-NYAH-nah ehs FYEHS-tah — is tomorrow a public holiday? Three words, asked the night before
Es fiesta local. — ehs FYEHS-tah loh-KAHL — 👂 it's a local holiday. Meaning here, and not in the next town

Standing in somebody else's street

A procession — a patron saint going out, a Holy Week brotherhood, a neighbourhood fiesta with a band — uses the street as the venue. There is no gate, no ticket, no seating and no inside: the route is published in advance by the town hall and the local press, the road closes while the thing passes, and the crowd is made of the people who live on it. Three small pieces of etiquette cover you completely. Stand still while it goes by rather than walking across the front of it. Cross behind, not through. And if you want to know what it is, ask the person next to you, because being asked is a small pleasure for somebody who has walked in it since they were a child. The one operational fact worth planning around is that a route through the centre can shut it to traffic for hours, taxis included, so a station or airport run on a fiesta afternoon goes early or goes underground. Finally, the other end of the year, which is the piece most British visitors have backwards: the Canary Islands run an inverted season, and November to February is their best and busiest stretch, while the Balearics are a May to October proposition. Booking the Canaries for August and the Balearics for January is the single most common way to get the wrong island at the wrong time.
