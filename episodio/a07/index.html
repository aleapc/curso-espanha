Two kisses, one hand, and why neither of them is a rule

The shape is simple and it is worth having in your head before you need it: two kisses, right cheek to right cheek first, then the left, cheeks touching or nearly touching and the lips landing on nothing. It is the private greeting — friends, family, a friend of a friend, somebody's flat, an introduction at a table — and it runs between women, and between a woman and a man. Two men who are not family shake hands, or do the hand-plus-shoulder version if they know each other well. What it is not is commercial: nobody behind a counter, a desk, a till or a hotel reception is going to offer it, and if you go in for it there you have simply misread which kind of room you are in. The piece that makes all of this manageable is that the hand settles it. Whoever puts a hand out first has chosen for both people, and the other person takes the hand without a flicker. So the get-out is total and you should use it whenever you want to: put your hand out, and nobody in Spain is offended, ever. One honest note on where this comes from. This is a description of a well-attested convention, not a measured finding — the best European observational work on greeting distance and touch does not include Spain at all, so anybody telling you that studies show Spaniards stand exactly so many centimetres closer is inventing the precision. What you have here is the behaviour and the way out of it, which is all you need at a door.

Mucho gusto. — MOO-choh GOOS-toh — pleased to meet you. Fits the hand and fits the cheeks, and never changes shape
Encantado. / Encantada. — en-kahn-TAH-dhoh — 👂 the one that comes back at you. Recognise it; you do not need to produce it

Closer, louder, and over the top

Three things about a Spanish table read to a British ear as something they are not. The distance is shorter, the volume is higher, and people talk on top of each other. None of the three is aggression, and the third one is the one that costs visitors the most, because the British conversational habit is to wait for a gap that is never going to open. A gap in that conversation does not mean 'your turn' — it means the subject has died, and somebody will now change it. The way in is to go over the top at ordinary volume, and there is a set of little openers for doing exactly that: 'oye', 'oye, una cosa', 'pues yo…', 'mira'. They are not rude and they are not clever; they are the ordinary machinery of taking a turn. The hand on the arm belongs to the same family. It arrives when the conversation is going well, it is a marker of engagement rather than of intimacy, and the single most useful thing you can do with it is nothing at all: stay where you are. Stepping back is a reply, whether you meant it as one or not, and it is read as wanting to be elsewhere. And on volume, one permission worth repeating from the first week: talking a bit louder than feels polite to you is what gets you heard, and lowering your voice in a loud bar is the thing that marks you out.

Oye, una cosa. — OH-yeh, OO-nah KOH-sah — listen, one thing. The floor, taken without a gap
Pues yo creo que… — pwehs yoh KREH-oh keh — 👂 well I reckon… The other standard way in, over the top of somebody

The two seconds, and the no that never gets said

Every room has an edge somewhere, and the way this one tells you that you have found it is a two-second silence followed by somebody starting a different subject and not looking at you while they do it. It is not anger, it is not a sanction, and it is finished by the time you have noticed it — the new subject is the repair, and it has been offered by somebody else so that nobody has to say what happened. The correct move is therefore the cheapest one available: take the new subject with interest and leave the old one lying there. An apology reopens the thing you were being let off, and now everybody has to have the conversation nobody wanted. The flat 'ya, ya' with the eyes elsewhere is the spoken version of the same two seconds. The near relative of all this is the refusal that is not phrased as one. 'Ya te diré' — I'll let you know — and 'ya veremos' — we'll see — are polite noes, and 'es complicado' is a third. None of them is a plan, and pressing for a date turns a comfortable no into an uncomfortable one that somebody now has to say to your face. 'Vale, otro día' releases them, keeps the friendship intact, and keeps you on the list for the invitation they actually do mean. The one place to be careful with this reading is commercial: 'ya le digo algo' from a workshop or an office can be a genuinely pending answer, and there it is fair to ring back.

Ya te diré. / Ya veremos. — yah teh dee-REH — 👂 I'll let you know / we'll see. Both are no, said kindly
Vale, otro día. — BAH-leh, OH-troh DEE-ah — all right, another day. The answer that keeps everybody

Guiri, and what the word is actually pointing at

You will hear it, probably in the third person and probably not aimed at you: guiri, the ordinary slang word for a northern-European visitor. It is teasing and it is usually fond; it is not an insult, and it is not a word anybody is going to use to your face as an attack. What it points at is not your nationality but a set of visible habits — the eleven-in-the-morning beer, the shirt off two streets from the sand, the socks and sandals, the sunburn, the wallet on the table. Which is the useful bit: it is a description of behaviour, and behaviour is adjustable in a way that a passport is not. Two things follow. First, hearing it is information rather than hostility, and reacting to it as an insult is itself the most guiri thing available in the moment. Second, the same word is doing a job for them that no English word quite does, halfway between 'tourist' and 'holidaymaker', and it is used about the whole category with a certain affection — the guiri, after all, is also the person paying for the terrace. Whether you make a joke of it about yourself is a separate skill, and it lives in a part of its own.

Mira, los guiris. — MEE-rah, lohs GEE-rees — 👂 look, the guiris. Overheard, not aimed
Somos guiris, ya lo sabemos. — SOH-mohs GEE-rees — 👂 we're guiris, we know. The shape it takes when it is said with a laugh

Leaving: the signal that starts it, and the twenty minutes after

The British reading of 'the evening is over' is built out of things that mean nothing here. The plates being cleared means the food has finished, not the evening — the hour after the plates is the sobremesa, and in a lot of houses it is the best hour of the night. The bill not arriving means the table is still yours, because bringing it unasked is read as throwing a customer out. A quiet minute means somebody is thinking. What actually ends it is physical and it is unmistakable once you know to look for it: the host gets to their feet, says something like 'bueno, pues nada', and starts moving plates or glasses. Before that, standing up gets you '¿ya te vas?', which is not a formality — it is an invitation to sit down again, and 'no, no, me quedo un rato' is the whole of the answer. After it, the leaving itself is a process rather than a moment: goodbyes in the room, more in the hall, more at the door, and a last one from the street, and it genuinely runs on for a good ten minutes. This is not politeness theatre to be escaped; it is the part where the invitation to come back gets made. The way to get it wrong in the other direction is to make the announcement and then keep sitting there, which reads as wanting to be persuaded. Announce it on your feet, with the jacket in your hand, and mean it — and then let the twenty minutes happen.

¿Ya te vas? — yah teh bahs — 👂 off already? An invitation to sit back down, not a question
Bueno, pues nada. — BWEH-noh, pwehs NAH-dhah — 👂 right then, that's that. Said standing. This one is the real signal
