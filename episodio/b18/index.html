'Me he dejado' and 'me han robado' go to different counters

Spanish keeps two clean sentences where English blurs one. 'Me he dejado el móvil en el bar' says the thing is where you put it and nobody did anything wrong; it is an appeal for help and the person hearing it has no reason to feel accused, which is exactly why they go and look. 'Me han robado el móvil' says somebody took it; it is the sentence a police report is written from, it is the one your insurer needs, and it is taught in part seventeen for that reason. Say the second one in a bar and you will usually get a defence rather than a search — not because anyone is touchy, but because you have just described a crime in their premises. The frame takes an object and a place, and the object list is short enough to hold in your head: el móvil, la chaqueta, las llaves, la maleta, el pasaporte. The places are en el taxi, en el tren, en la habitación, en el bar, and the shortest of all, aquí. Add '¿lo han encontrado?' straight afterwards — 'has anyone found it?' — and the sentence stops being an announcement and becomes a request. If the thing is feminine, the little word changes to 'la han encontrado', and nobody has ever failed to find a jacket because a visitor said 'lo' instead of 'la'.

Me he dejado el pasaporte en la habitación. — meh eh deh-KHAH-dhoh el pah-sah-POR-teh — I've left my passport in the room.
Me he dejado una cosa aquí. ¿La han encontrado? — OO-nah KOH-sah ah-KEE — I've left a thing here. Has anyone found it? (with the photo on your screen, this is a complete sentence)

Check-out, and the tax that is not on your booking

Three sentences do the desk: 'Ya está pagado' if you settled online, '¿Me puede dar la factura, por favor?' for the itemised bill with the property's name on it, and '¿Puedo dejar las maletas hasta las seis?' for the gap between the room and the flight. Note that leaving the cases is a normal request and not a favour — the luggage room exists because everybody's flight is at nine. The one thing that surprises people is the nightly tourist tax. It exists in Catalonia, the Balearics and, since 2026, the Basque Country; the property collects it on behalf of the region, it is generally not inside what you paid on a booking site, and it is often asked for in cash at the desk on the way out. In most of Spain there is no such tax at all, which is why it feels like an invention when it appears. The defence is not an argument, it is a question asked on the first morning — '¿La tasa turística está incluida?' — so that on the last morning it is a line you were expecting. Barcelona sits at the top end of the scale, where the total for a five-star hotel reached twelve euros a night in April 2026 and the municipal part of it is on a published schedule to keep rising to 2029.

¿Se paga aparte la tasa? — seh PAH-gah ah-PAR-teh — Is the tax paid separately?
¿Puedo dejar las maletas hasta las seis? — deh-KHAR lahs mah-LEH-tahs — Can I leave the cases until six?

The ride out, the hire car, and the machine at the border

Going out is the same conversation as coming in, with the questions in the same order: '¿Está libre?', the address on the screen rather than in your accent, and the fare settled before the boot shuts. In Madrid there is nothing to negotiate — a taxi between Barajas and any address inside the M-30 is a fixed thirty-three euros, twenty-four hours a day, with no surcharges permitted, which is why '¿Es tarifa fija al aeropuerto?' is a confirmation rather than a haggle. If you are returning a hire car, the two things that cost money are the tank and the paperwork: fill it before you arrive if the contract is full-to-full, and when the man with the tablet says everything is in order, ask for that in writing — '¿Me lo puede dar por escrito, por favor?' The pressure at that counter is verbal by design, and a sentence in writing is the only kind that survives the drive home. Ask also when the deposit comes back off the card. At the border, the same system that photographed you and took four fingerprints on the way in records your exit; the profile already exists, so the second pass is much faster than the first. It also means the ninety-days-in-any-one-hundred-and-eighty count is now automatic, and your exit date is part of it.

¿Cuándo me lo devuelven? — KWAN-doh meh loh deh-BWEL-behn — When do I get it back? (the deposit blocked on your card)
¿Es lleno a lleno? — es YEH-noh ah YEH-noh — Is it full-to-full? (the only fuel policy worth having)

Saying goodbye to people who served you all week

The last thing is the cheapest thing in the course and the one most visitors skip: a sentence to the bar that fed you every morning. 'Ha estado genial' — it's been great — and 'Volveré' — I'll come back. Add 'Muchas gracias por todo' at the hotel desk and 'Hasta la próxima' on the way out of the door, and you have done in fifteen seconds what a card and a tip do not do. 'Hasta luego' is the ordinary goodbye and it does not commit you to returning later that day — it is what you say on the way out of a shop, even to someone you will never see again, and 'Adiós' does the same job. Keep 'en absoluto' out of your mouth entirely on this last morning: it looks like 'absolutely' and it means 'not in the slightest', so the enthusiastic answer to '¿Le ha gustado?' turns into a flat no in front of the person who cooked it. What you want is '¡Totalmente!' or 'Desde luego'. And if you are asked whether you will be back, the honest answer, said out loud, tends to be the one that gets remembered next year.

Hasta la próxima. — AHS-tah lah PROK-see-mah — Until the next time.
Que os vaya muy bien. — keh ohs BAH-yah mwee byen — All the best to you (said to the pair of them behind the counter)
