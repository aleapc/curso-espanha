Why there is no speaking in this one

Your brain recognises far more than it can produce, and the gap between the two is the cheapest thing in the whole course to close. You already own the sentences; what you do not own yet is the ability to spot them when a third of the sounds have gone. So this part spends its twelve minutes entirely on the receiving side, and it is the only part of the course where you are asked to say nothing at all. That is deliberate and it is not a day off: recognising a worn-down sentence is a different skill from producing a careful one, and the only way to build it is exposure to the worn-down version of things you already know. This is also why every single sentence in here came out of an earlier part. A new sentence would have made it a vocabulary lesson with a difficult accent on top, and you would have learned neither. And to be plain about the ambition: you will never need to speak like this. You will absolutely need to understand it, because it starts exactly where the hotel English stops.

¿Me pones dos cañas, por favor? → ¿Me poneh doh cañah, por favó? — the same order twice — the second one is the one you will actually be standing next to
El pescado está muy bueno. → El pescao está mu bueno. — the fish is very good — same fish, same price, one letter shorter

The four things that happen to fast Spanish, in the order you meet them

One: the S at the end of a syllable turns into a soft puff of air and then into nothing, so dos cervezas comes out closer to 'doh thervetha'. Two: the 'th' of gracias and cerveza is a plain S in some places — that is seseo, and the RAE and ASALE describe it as standard in the Canaries, in parts of Andalusia and throughout the Americas, with the 'th' distinction holding across most of the rest of peninsular Spain. Three: the D between two vowels drops out, so pescado becomes pescao, cansado becomes cansao, pagado becomes pagao — and this one is ordinary relaxed speech all over Spain, not a southern thing. Four: the R and the L at the end of a word soften, so pagar becomes pagá and para llevar becomes pa' llevá. Item two has a primary source behind it; items one, three and four are the settled description you will find in any dialectology of Spanish, but we have not put a single primary citation next to them, and this is us saying so rather than dressing it up. Nothing in the audio depends on the difference: what the audio asks of you is to recognise your own sentences with holes in them, and holes are holes whoever put them there.

Ya está pagado. → Ya 'tá pagao. — it is already paid — two holes in four words, and it still settles the bill
Para llevar, por favor. → Pa' llevá, por favó. — to take away, please — the R on the end going, twice in one breath

Two different people, not two accents — and why we are being careful about that

The careful recordings in this part are the same voices you have been learning from since part one. The fast recordings are two other people, and that is the honest description of what changed: another person, another rhythm, another speed, the same Spanish. We are deliberately not telling you that the second voice is from Seville, or from anywhere else, because we have not verified that it is, and a course that tells you what a region sounds like on the strength of a recording it did not check is teaching you an accent that may not exist. The distinction matters for a practical reason too. If you file this away as 'southern Spanish', you will switch the skill off the moment you are in the north — and the fast version is what you get at a busy counter in any city in the country, at nine at night, from somebody who has four orders in his head. The rule that survives all of it is the one the hand has been teaching you since part one: the number of beats does not change. Count those and you will find the sentence even when half the consonants have gone.

¿Cuántas cosas más? → ¿Cuánta' cosa' má'? — anything else? — five beats either way, three fewer letters
¿Dónde están los aseos? → ¿Dónde 'tán loh aseo'? — where are the toilets? — the front of están goes as well as the ends

The one thing you should never do with any of this

Never correct anybody. If somebody says 'grasias' where you were taught 'grathias', they are speaking their own language in their own way and there is nothing to fix. The same goes for the dropped S and the missing D: they are not sloppiness and they are not an error, any more than a Londoner saying 'wa'er' has misspelled water. Correcting a native speaker's own pronunciation is the single fastest way to turn a friendly counter into a cold one, and it is a mistake almost exclusively made by people who have just finished a language course. The useful reflex in the other direction is the one from part three: when you genuinely have not got it, you do not ask for it again — again gets you the same speed, one notch louder — you ask for it slower, by name. And when even that fails, take the one word you did catch, use it, and let the rest go. Understanding four words in five is not a partial result at a bar. It is how everybody there is listening, including them.

¿Me puede hablar más despacio, por favor? — could you speak more slowly, please — asks for speed, not for repetition
¿Cómo? — 👂 sorry? — what you will hear them use on each other, and it gets the sentence back at the same speed
