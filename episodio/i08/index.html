¿Nos vemos…? — the two sentences that turn a nice thought into a Thursday

An invitation in Spain very often arrives without a time attached to it: 'vente a cenar', 'te vienes un día a casa', 'tenemos que quedar'. That is not vagueness and it is not a polite brush-off — it is an opening, and whoever is keener puts the hour on it. The sentence that does that is '¿Nos vemos a las nueve en la plaza?' Literally it is 'do we see each other at nine in the square', and it is a question only in the sense that a handshake is a question. The front of it — nos vemos — never changes; what you drop in is an hour and a place, in that order, with 'en' in front of the place. Two neighbours are worth having whole. '¿Quedamos allí?' is the three-word version for when the place has already been named out loud and neither of you fancies saying it again; quedar is the ordinary verb for arranging to meet, and '¿quedamos?' on its own is what a Spanish phone message looks like. And 'vale, allí estaré' is the yes: vale is the single most useful word in the country, somewhere between 'right', 'okay' and 'fine by me'. If the other person moves the hour on you — '¿y si quedamos a las diez?' — the whole answer is those three words. Do not build a longer one.

¿Nos vemos mañana en el bar de abajo? — nohs BEH-mohs mah-NYAH-nah — shall we meet tomorrow at the bar downstairs?
Vale, quedamos allí. — BAH-leh, keh-DHAH-mohs ah-YEE — right, we'll meet there. The statement version of the question

What goes through the door, and why it is never opened

The rule is simple and it is the only one anybody would notice you getting wrong: you do not arrive at somebody's home with nothing in your hands. What you take is deliberately small — a bottle of wine, a cake or a tart from the bakery on the corner, a box of something for the children, flowers if you like the person a lot. It is a greeting rather than a contribution to the meal, and that is why it will very often go straight onto a shelf, unopened: the wine for tonight was decided before you rang the bell, and yours is for another day. Do not read that as a snub and do not offer to open it. The sentence that goes with the handover is 'te he traído una cosita' — I've brought you a little something — and the diminutive on cosita is doing real work: it says the thing is small on purpose, which takes any weight of obligation out of it. If you are handing it to somebody you are on usted with, 'le he traído una cosita'. On price: nobody is going to look, and a supermarket bottle handed over warmly beats an expensive one handed over stiffly. The one thing worth avoiding is arriving with a whole course you have made, unless it was asked for — that reads as a comment on the cooking.

Te he traído una cosita para los niños. — PAH-rah lohs NEE-nyohs — I've brought a little something for the children
Es un vino de mi tierra. — deh mee TYEH-rrah — it's a wine from where I'm from. The line that makes a cheap bottle a good present

Nine means nine, and the fifteen minutes nobody writes down

Dinner at home is late by British standards and the number is not a rounding error: 'sobre las nueve' means the table happens somewhere around then, and turning up at nine exactly is turning up early. A quarter of an hour after the hour is the ordinary arrival, and it is what the host has planned for — the shower, the last pan, the tidying of the room you will be sat in. This is the one place in the whole course where being punctual is the mistake, and it runs the other way for everything with a timetable: the train, the museum slot, the doctor and a table booked in a restaurant are all to the minute. The wording tells you which world you are in. 'A las nueve' is an appointment. 'Sobre las nueve', 'a eso de las nueve' or 'a partir de las nueve' is a home, and the last of those three genuinely means 'from nine onwards'. One more piece of arithmetic worth having: if you are being fed at somebody's house, do not eat at seven to be safe. You will not be hungry, they will notice, and the whole point of the evening is the table.

A eso de las nueve, ¿te va bien? — ah EH-soh deh lahs NWEH-beh — around nine, does that suit you?
A partir de las nueve, cuando quieras. — ah par-TEER deh — from nine onwards, whenever you like

The second helping, the second no, and the hour after the plates

Two things at the table have a shape you can plan for. The first is the offer of more, which runs twice: '¿un poquito más?', 'venga, que hay de sobra', and a spoonful may well arrive while you are still saying no. Nobody is ignoring you — the first refusal is heard as manners, and the answer is the second one. Make it land by putting the reason in the same breath: 'de verdad que no puedo más, estaba todo buenísimo', with a flat hand over the plate. That sentence is the one from part twenty-five with 'todo' dropped into the middle of it, and it does two jobs at once: it closes the food and it compliments the cook, which is why nobody can be offended by it. There is one exception and it is absolute: an allergy or a diet never goes into this soft register. That is the flat, serious sentence from part thirteen, said looking at them, with the card in your hand. The second shape is what happens after the plates: nothing. The coffee comes out, sometimes something stronger, and the table sits on. This has its own name — la sobremesa — and it is not the polite tail of the evening, it is the evening. Leaving as the plates are cleared is the single most common way a visitor turns a warm night into a cool one, entirely by accident.

Está riquísimo, pero no puedo más. — PEH-roh noh PWEH-dhoh mahs — it's delicious, but I can't manage any more
¿Nos quedamos un rato de sobremesa? — oon RRAH-toh — shall we sit on a bit after the food?

Getting out of the door: say it twice, and the three set wishes

Leaving is a two-stage thing and knowing that saves the whole ending. The first announcement — 'bueno, me voy yendo, que mañana madrugo' — is not a departure, it is notice. 'Me voy yendo' is literally 'I'm going going', a shape that means the process has started rather than finished, and 'madrugar' is the verb for getting up early, which is the standard and completely accepted excuse for anything. Expect nobody to move. Ten minutes later you stand up, and that one is real. On your feet at the door, the whole goodbye is 'muchas gracias por todo, ha estado genial', and if you want to be asked back, 'a ver si repetimos' — let's do it again — is what people actually say. Three set wishes cover the rest of the night and none of them is taken apart: '¡salud!' with the glass, 'que aproveche' at anyone eating, and 'que vaya bien' to somebody heading off. They all begin with that little 'que' because they are old wishes with the front of the sentence worn away — 'I hope that it goes well' — and you never need to know that to use them. The family is bigger if you want it: 'que descanses' to somebody going to bed, 'que te mejores' to somebody ill, 'que tengas suerte' before an exam. Same shape, all of it learnt whole.

A ver si repetimos. — ah behr see rreh-peh-TEE-mohs — let's do this again. The line that gets you invited back
Que descanses. — keh dehs-KAHN-sehs — 👂 sleep well, to somebody heading for bed
