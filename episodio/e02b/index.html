EES in plain English

The Entry/Exit System has been fully operational at the external Schengen border since 10 April 2026, replacing the passport stamp across 29 European countries. It records your name, document details, facial image, four fingerprints, and every entry and exit — including refusals of entry. It is free and there is no form to fill in. First registration at a self-service kiosk has been reported at roughly four minutes per person, which is why the April rollout produced multi-hour queues at some European hubs before airports adjusted. Subsequent crossings reuse your stored profile and are much faster. Member states are permitted to run a hybrid — kiosks plus legacy stamped booths — and to suspend biometric capture temporarily under exceptional pressure, so what you meet on the day depends on the airport and the hour. Field guides through 2026 have been recommending three hours before a long-haul departure rather than the usual two; treat that as other travellers' advice, not an official rule.

The 90/180 rule, properly explained

British and American visitors may stay in the Schengen area for up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period. 'Rolling' is the word people miss: there is no calendar reset. From any given day, look back 180 days and total the days you were inside the area — day of entry and day of exit both count as full days. Since EES removed the stamp, that total is now computed automatically from a central record. Overstaying can bring a ban of up to three years. Owning property in Spain confers no immigration right at all: a villa owner in Marbella has exactly the same 90 days as anyone else. If you want longer, that is a visa question, and it needs sorting out at home, months in advance.

Why check-in asks for everything short of your blood type

Royal Decree 933/2021 has, since 2 December 2024, obliged Spanish accommodation providers and vehicle hire companies to collect and transmit to the security forces: full name, document or passport number, nationality, sex, date of birth, telephone, email, full home address, number of travellers, and the relationship between them where minors are involved. It applies to hotels, tourist apartments and car hire counters alike, sometimes on a form printed only in Spanish. It is a legal requirement, not a data grab by the property — and if you decline to supply it, they cannot check you in.

The 'Me alojo en…' swap

'Me alojo en…' means 'I'm staying at…' and takes any kind of address, which makes it the only version of this answer you need to memorise. Say the frame, then drop in your accommodation. If you're asked in the more informal register you'll hear '¿Dónde te alojas?'; from an officer it will be the formal '¿Dónde se aloja?' — and that is one of the three places in Spain where 'usted' is genuinely the right register.

Me alojo en un apartamento, en el centro. — meh ah-LOH-khoh en oon ah-par-tah-MEN-toh, en el THEN-troh — I'm staying in a flat, in the centre.
Me alojo en casa de un amigo. — meh ah-LOH-khoh en KAH-sah deh oon ah-MEE-goh — I'm staying at a friend's house.

The other things the border can ask for

Spain's published entry requirements allow officers to ask for a return or onward ticket, proof of travel insurance, evidence of sufficient funds, and confirmation of your accommodation or the address where you'll be staying. The funds figure is set at 10% of the Spanish minimum wage per person per day with an overall floor of 90% of it — around €122 a day and roughly €1,099 minimum in 2026 — and is proven with cash, traveller's cheques or a credit card together with a bank statement. In reality this is very rarely requested of British or American arrivals on holiday routes. Know it exists, keep the booking on your phone, and don't build a panic out of it.
