Why the insult is the compliment

Linguists who work on recordings of everyday Spanish conversation have a name for what happens at that table: mock impoliteness. The form of the utterance is an attack — you are a donkey, you are useless, shut up — and the function is the opposite of an attack. It says: our relationship is strong enough that I can say this to your face in front of five people, and you and everybody else know it will hold. That is why it gets spent on the person the speaker is surest of, and why answering in kind is the polite move rather than the rude one. Two consequences follow, and only the second one matters at your table. First: the words are not a vocabulary list to learn, because the meaning is not in them. Second, and this is the whole reason the part exists: the reading depends entirely on the history between the two people, and a visitor in week one has none. Identical words, identical tone, and a completely different landing. This is not a rule about being careful — it is a rule about what you are holding. You are holding no history yet, so you hold the laugh instead, which is free and which works from the first evening. If you want the reading, look for the pragmatics literature on Spanish conversation corpora under 'mock impoliteness' and 'descortesía de fustigación'; the audio deliberately never mentions any of it, because the behaviour is the useful part and the label is not.

¡Pero qué burro eres, tío! — PEH-roh keh BOO-rroh EH-rehs TEE-oh — 👂 literally 'what a donkey you are', and between old friends it is closer to 'you idiot, I love you'
Anda, calla. — AN-dah KAH-yah — 👂 'oh, be quiet' — the standard answer in kind, and the sound of nobody being offended

Anda ya, venga ya, and the two-word family

'Anda ya' is the one to own. It is disbelief with a smile on it, and it covers everything from a tall story to a wind-up aimed at you: get away with you, do me a favour, pull the other one. Its neighbours are 'venga ya' (same job, slightly more forceful), 'no me digas' (you don't say — genuine surprise rather than disbelief) and 'qué va' (nothing of the sort, used to knock down a suggestion rather than a person). '¡Qué me estás contando!' is the big one and it is aimed at the story, never at the teller, which is exactly why it is safe: you are marvelling, not accusing. You will also hear '¡no me lo puedo creer!' and, in younger company, 'ni de coña' — worth recognising, not worth producing, because the second one is mildly rude in the way that is charming from a local and odd from a visitor. Notice what all of these have in common: they are short, they are said fast, and none of them requires you to have understood the whole story. That is the point. They are the sound of somebody who is in the conversation, which is a different thing from somebody who has followed it.

Venga ya, hombre. — BEN-gah yah OM-breh — 👂 come off it — the same move with a bit more push behind it
¿No me digas? — noh meh DEE-gahs — 👂 you don't say? — genuine surprise, and a perfectly good thing to say back to anybody

Guiri, and what to do with it

Guiri is the ordinary word for a visitor from northern Europe. It is not a slur, it is not usually said with any hostility, and it is very often said with affection — it lives in the same register as the English 'tourist' when your friend says it about you in a queue. What it points at is a look and a set of habits rather than a nationality: the shorts in October, the shoulders the colour of a postbox, the pint at eleven in the morning, sandals with socks. Which is precisely why the sentence 'soy el guiri, ya lo sé' works so well: you are not claiming an identity, you are agreeing with an observation about your shorts, and you are getting there first. Said once, on the way in, it does more than any amount of careful Spanish, because it tells the room you are not going to need managing. Two things it does not do. It is not an apology, so do not add 'lo siento' to it. And it is not a licence to use the word about anybody else — about another visitor it is odd, and about the person serving you it makes no sense at all. Related things you may be called and can safely enjoy: 'inglés' applied to every English-speaker regardless of passport, and 'rubio' or 'rubia' used as a friendly form of address at a market stall whatever colour your hair actually is.

Soy el guiri, ya lo sé. — soy el GEE-ree yah loh SEH — I'm the tourist, I know. Say it once, on the way in, with a shrug
Hablo un poco de español. Muy poco. — AH-bloh oon POH-koh deh ehs-pah-NYOL, mwee POH-koh — the sentence from part three with the joke bolted on the end

Tío, hombre, and the words that are not really words

Three of the most common noises at a Spanish table are not carrying meaning at all, and mistaking them for meaning is what makes an ordinary conversation sound like an argument. 'Tío' and 'tía' mean uncle and aunt in a family and mean nothing whatsoever between friends — they are the tag on the end of a sentence, roughly the job that 'mate' does in Britain, and they are everywhere. 'Hombre' is not addressing a man: '¡hombre!' on its own is pleased surprise at somebody arriving, 'hombre, no' is a gentle disagreement, and it gets said to women constantly. 'Venga' is the all-purpose closer — right then, come on, off we go, see you — and it ends about a third of the phone calls in the country. None of these are worth producing on purpose in your first fortnight; all of them are worth recognising, because once you stop hearing them as content the sentences underneath get a great deal shorter and a great deal clearer. And one about volume, since it belongs here: a Spanish table at full tilt is louder than a British one, and several people talking at once is not people interrupting each other. Waiting politely for a gap is how a visitor ends up saying nothing for two hours.

Que está de cachondeo, hombre. — keh ehs-TAH deh kah-chon-DEH-oh OM-breh — 👂 he's winding you up — the sentence somebody kind says for your benefit
Venga, hasta luego. — BEN-gah AHS-tah LWEH-goh — 👂 right then, see you — the standard end of almost anything

The fence, and how to walk away from it without limping

The other half of this part is the one nobody warns you about, because it looks like good manners. Somebody who has been on tú with you all evening switches to usted; the sentences get short and correct; the voice flattens out; 'como usted quiera' and 'lo que usted diga' start appearing. That is not respect arriving, it is a door being closed politely, and in a conversation that was easy a minute ago the switch itself is the message. Most often it is not about you personally: you have walked into a subject that the table has decided is not for tonight, which is what the part on the old argument, later in this module, is entirely about. What to do is small and it has to be quick — change the subject in your very next sentence, keep your volume and your face exactly where they were, and do not apologise, because apologising names the thing and makes it bigger. Give it a minute and the temperature comes back on its own. The mirror image is worth holding on to as well: if somebody has used usted with you from the first word — a pharmacist, an older waiter, a hotel desk, anybody in a uniform — then usted is simply the register they work in and it carries no message at all. The signal is the change, never the form.

Como usted quiera. — KOH-moh oos-TEHDH KYEH-rah — 👂 as you like — polite, correct, and a closed door if it turns up mid-conversation
Bueno, cambiando de tema… — BWEH-noh, kam-BYAN-doh deh TEH-mah — 👂 anyway, changing the subject — you will hear somebody else do this for you, and you can do it too
