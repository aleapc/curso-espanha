Why the goodbye takes ten minutes

The thing to notice is not that people are slow to leave — it is that leaving is treated as a stretch of the evening with its own shape, rather than as the moment the evening stops. It opens with somebody saying 'bueno', usually with a hand on a knee, and 'bueno' here is doing the work that a chair scraping does elsewhere: it announces, and nothing more. What follows is a run of second thoughts, last stories and the things nobody said over dinner, and it is very often the best part of the night. Then somebody actually stands, and the whole table moves at once. If you get up on the first 'bueno' you have not been efficient, you have taken the last ten minutes off five people, and the evening ends looking like a meeting. The practical version: announce it, stay seated, and leave when the second or third goodbye comes round. Useful noises for the run-up, none of which you have to produce: 'venga' (come on, right then), 'que ya es tarde' (it's late now), 'que mañana madrugo' (I've an early start). The exceptions are all clock-shaped — a work lunch, a bar stacking chairs, a last metro — and there the first goodbye is the real one.

Bueno, pues nada. — BWEH-noh, pwehs NAH-dhah — 👂 right then, well — the opening of the goodbye, not the end of it
Venga, que mañana madrugo. — BEN-gah — 👂 come on, I've an early start tomorrow

'Hasta luego', 'hasta la próxima' and 'nos vemos' are three different sizes

'Hasta luego' looks as though it means 'see you later' and commits you to a time. It does not. It is the ordinary word for bye, it is said walking out of a shop to somebody you will never see again, and it is what you say nine times a day for a fortnight. 'Adiós' does the same job and is slightly flatter; in some places it is even used as a greeting in passing. 'Hasta la próxima' — until the next time — is a size up: it is for somebody you have had a week with, and it says there will be a next time. 'Nos vemos' is the same weight and looser in shape: 'nos vemos el año que viene', 'nos vemos en septiembre', 'nos vemos pronto'. And 'que os vaya bien' — or 'que te vaya bien' to one person — is a wish rather than a farewell, said to people who are going off to do something: a shift, a drive, the rest of their week. All of these are learnt whole. Nothing inside them needs to be worked out at a door and nothing inside them ever gets taken apart: 'que te vaya bien' and 'que os vaya bien' are two separate things to carry, one aimed at a person and one at a pair.

Nos vemos en septiembre. — nohs BEH-mohs en sep-TYEM-breh — see you in September
Que te vaya bien. — keh teh BAH-yah byen — all the best (to one person)

The open invitation, and taking it at face value

'Cuando volváis, ya sabéis dónde estamos' — whenever you're back, you know where we are — is not a form of words to be nodded at. In a bar or a shop you have used every day for a week, it is closer to an address being handed over: no date is expected, but the sentence is meant, and answering it with a thank you rather than a when is a small missed opportunity. The reply that works is a plan without a diary entry: 'nos vemos el año que viene'. It costs nothing, it is heard as true, and it is the reason the second coffee turns up unasked the following summer. This runs exactly opposite to the British habit of saying 'we must do this again sometime' as a way of ending a conversation. Said in Spain, that sentence is taken literally, and the honest thing is either to mean it or to use a smaller goodbye instead. One more that belongs in the same drawer: if somebody says 'a ver si nos vemos' — let's see if we can meet up — that is a genuine, if soft, opening, and the way to accept it is to name something, however small.

A ver si nos vemos. — ah behr see nohs BEH-mohs — 👂 let's see if we can meet up — soft, and real
Ya sabéis dónde estamos. — yah sah-BAIS DHON-deh ehs-TAH-mohs — 👂 you know where we are

Saying goodbye to the people who served you all week

The counter you stood at every morning is the cheapest relationship of the trip and the one most visitors leave without touching. The reason is structural rather than rude: he does not know it is your last day. A week of nods only becomes a week at the moment somebody says out loud that it is ending. Three short sentences do all of it, standing up, with the case by the door: 'Mañana nos vamos' — we leave tomorrow; 'Ha estado genial' — it's been great; 'Volveré' or 'nos vemos el año que viene'. Add 'gracias por la paciencia con mi español' for whoever slowed down for you and never switched to English, and expect to be told your Spanish is excellent, which it is not and which is not the point. If you were served by two people, 'que os vaya muy bien' takes them both at once. None of this is a speech: the whole thing is under fifteen seconds, and it is the difference between being a customer and being a face. Where it does not apply is a big place with a rota — twenty staff, a queue behind you, nobody who has served you twice. There a nod is genuinely the whole of what is on offer, and nothing has gone wrong.

Mañana nos vamos. Ha estado genial. — mah-NYAH-nah nohs BAH-mohs — we're off tomorrow. It's been great
Gracias por la paciencia con mi español. — pah-THYEN-thyah — thanks for putting up with my Spanish

The twelve things, written down

The pocket list, in full, for the screenshot, in the order the recap says it. One: dinner runs from about nine, and last orders are around half eleven; at seven you are having tapas, which is a good evening and not the one you booked. Two: the 'menú del día' at weekday lunch averages about €14.20 and is the best value in the country — and while you are at that table, a free tapa with the drink is a Granada habit rather than a Spanish law, so expect nothing and be pleased by everything. Three: 'una jarra de agua del grifo, por favor' — tap water inside a hospitality business has been free by law since 2022. Four: 'de guardia' is the word — there is no chemist open around the clock on every corner, there is a rota, and tonight's list is posted on the door of the closed one. Five: check the printed price list before you sit on the terrace; bar, table and terrace are legally allowed to cost three different amounts. Six: round up; a fifth does not read as generous. Seven: bag in front of you on the metro and phone off the table, particularly on the busiest lines at rush hour. Eight: do not take the sprig of rosemary — hands in pockets, 'no, gracias', keep walking. Nine: real officers do not ask to see your wallet; 'vamos a la comisaría' ends that conversation. Ten: 'la hoja de reclamaciones, por favor', said calmly and sitting down, settles most arguments before it is ever filled in. Eleven: T-shirt on when you leave the sand, can off the street, and in Barcelona that fine is now taken on the spot, by card. Twelve, and the cheapest of the lot: 'hola' walking in and 'hasta luego' walking out, every door, every time, including the doors you will never walk through again. Rules change, so check the official page before you fly — the links are in the app.

Una jarra de agua del grifo, por favor. — OO-nah KHAH-rrah deh AH-gwah del GREE-foh — a jug of tap water, please
La hoja de reclamaciones, por favor. — lah OH-khah deh rreh-klah-mah-THYOH-nehs — the complaint form, please
