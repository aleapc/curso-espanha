The question that comes back small

An open question gets an open answer, and an open answer in Spanish at counter speed is eleven seconds you cannot hold on to. '¿Qué me recomienda?' is a perfectly good sentence that hands the whole board back to a stranger and asks him to describe it. '¿Cuál me recomienda: el arroz o el pescado?' hands him two things and takes one back. That is the entire design: you are not testing your comprehension, you are shrinking the answer until it fits. The front never moves — cuál me recomienda — and what changes is the two things, which you read off the board, off the shelf or off the next table. Four endings cover the trip: two things joined by 'o'; '¿cuál está mejor hoy?' when the counter changes daily; '¿cuál es más típico de aquí?' when you want the local one rather than the popular one; and '¿este es mejor que aquel?' when you are holding both. If you are wondering why it is cuál and not qué — Spanish reaches for cuál when the choice is between things already in front of you, and qué when you are asking for a category. You will never need to know that at a table. Pick the version with the two things in it and the question sorts itself out. One more thing you can safely ignore: we recorded 'recomienda', the version for somebody behind a counter you do not know. In a young bar you will hear 'recomiendas' flying about, and if somebody uses that with you, use it back. Nothing here breaks either way.

¿Cuál me llevo, el manchego o el cabrales? — KWAL meh YEH-boh — which shall I take, the manchego or the cabrales? (the version for a shop counter)
¿Este es mejor que aquel? — EHS-teh es meh-KHOR keh ah-KEL — is this one better than that one? (when you are holding both)

El menú del día — what you get, what it costs, and the myth to bin

A starter, a main, bread, a drink and pudding or coffee, for one fixed price, on weekdays at lunchtime. The national average was about €14.20 in 2026 (Hostelería de España with Edenred), and it moves by region: cheapest in the Canaries, Asturias, Andalusia and Murcia, dearest in the Balearics. It was €11.70 in 2016, which means it has risen more slowly than the cost of everything else. It is the best value in Spanish eating, and most English-speaking visitors walk straight past it because they are scanning for the words 'set menu' and what is written on the board is 'menú'. Correction worth carrying: no law in force obliges a restaurant to offer one. The requirement dates from the Franco era and was repealed, so any guide that tells you every restaurant must have one is out of date. It survives because it works — for the kitchen, which cooks two things well and in quantity, and for the customer. Two consequences you can act on. It is lunch, not dinner: la comida runs from about two to half three or four, and by mid-afternoon the offer has gone with it. And 'menú' is not 'menu' — the list of everything else is la carta, so 'la carta, por favor' gets you the full list and 'el menú, por favor' gets you today's three plates.

¿Hay menú del día? — ai meh-NOO del DEE-ah — is there a set lunch on?
¿El menú lleva postre o café? — el meh-NOO YEH-bah POHS-treh oh kah-FEH — does the set lunch come with pudding or coffee? (usually one or the other, your choice)

Tapa, pincho, ración, media ración — and why the table filled up

Four words, four different sizes, and getting them the wrong way round is how two people end up with food for six. A tapa is the small plate that goes with a drink — free with the round in eastern Andalusia and a paid item in Barcelona and Madrid, which is geography and not law, and which part eleven covers in full. A pincho or montadito is the northern version, usually on a slice of bread and charged by the piece. A ración is a full sharing plate built for the middle of the table, and it is the one that surprises people: four raciones between two of you is not four small dishes, it is dinner for a family. A media ración is half of one, it is completely normal to ask for, and it is what lets two people try four things instead of two. Sharing from the centre is the default rather than a special occasion, so nobody will look twice if you reach across. The exception is the menú del día, which is plated per person: your starter, your main, and asking to share it just confuses the waiter. And if you cannot tell which size is in front of you, there is one question that settles it before anything is cooked.

¿Esto es tapa, media ración o ración? — EHS-toh es TAH-pah, MEH-dhyah rrah-THYON oh rrah-THYON — is this a tapa, a half portion or a full one?
Una media ración, para probar. — OO-nah MEH-dhyah rrah-THYON, PAH-rah proh-BAR — a half portion, just to try it

Reading a board you cannot read

You are not going to translate a handwritten chalkboard in the ninety seconds you have before somebody comes over, and you do not need to. Ten words repeat on almost every board in the country, and they are worth more than a dictionary: de primero and de segundo are the two courses, de postre is the pudding, del día is today's, de la casa is the house one, casero is home-made, a la plancha is off the griddle, al horno is baked, frito is fried, and rebozado means it has been battered. Everything else is a noun you can survive not knowing, because three sentences pick up the slack: '¿lleva…?' opens a dish before it arrives, 'lo mismo' plus a finger at the next table orders something you cannot name, and the frame from this part turns the whole board into a choice between two. What none of this requires is understanding all of it. Nobody at that table is marking your comprehension, and the waiter shutting the board and naming one dish is not a trick question — it is the shortest recommendation he has. Say yes to it. The worst case is a plate you would not have picked, which is precisely the plate this part is named after.

Lo mismo que ellos, por favor. — loh MEES-moh keh EH-yohs — the same as them, please (said with a finger at the next table)
¿Y de postre, qué hay? — ee deh POHS-treh, keh ai — and for pudding, what is there?
