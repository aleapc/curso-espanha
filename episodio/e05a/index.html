What dynamic currency conversion actually is

When a card machine or a cash point offers to charge you in pounds or dollars instead of euros, the exchange rate is being set by the merchant's payment provider, not by your bank — and the markup typically runs 10% to 13%. EU Regulation 2019/518, in force since 19 April 2020, obliges whoever offers the conversion to display the total cost as a percentage markup over the European Central Bank reference rate BEFORE you confirm. So the number is on the screen: look for it, then choose euros anyway. A documented example: €600 withdrawn from a Euronet machine with the conversion accepted cost roughly €80 extra once markup and fees were added. The skill being trained here is not translation — it is interrupting and refusing under mild social pressure, which is why the drill puts a queue behind you and a finger on the button.

Voy a pagar con tarjeta. — boy ah pah-GAR kon tar-KHEH-tah — level zero, said before the terminal is even switched on
En euros, por favor. — en EH-oo-rohs, por fah-BOR — level 1, said before the machine reaches you
Perdone, en euros. Sin conversión. — pehr-DOH-neh, seen kon-behr-SYON — level 2, when the screen already shows pounds
No, gracias. Cóbreme en euros, en moneda local. Sin conversión. — KOH-breh-meh en EH-oo-rohs — level 3, when they insist

Cash-only is legal, and so is the card minimum

There is no legal obligation in Spain for a business to accept cards. A bar displaying SOLO EFECTIVO is operating perfectly lawfully, and a small bar may also impose a card minimum as long as it is displayed. In Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia contactless is the default and most places have abandoned the old €5 minimum, but cash still rules in traditional bakeries, small pharmacies, family guesthouses, small family tapas bars in Andalusia, fruit stalls, craft stands and street markets. The practical answer is not to choose a side: carry €40–60 per person in small notes and ask before you order. Getting the notes out of a machine — which bank, which street, and why you take more out less often — is the first-hour part of this course, not this one. The cash point asks the conversion question too, and the answer there is identical.

¿Aceptan tarjeta o solo efectivo? — ah-THEP-tan tar-KHEH-tah oh SOH-loh eh-fek-TEE-boh
¿Hay un mínimo para pagar con tarjeta? — ai oon MEE-nee-moh — is there a card minimum?

Why the bill never comes, and what may legally be on it

Bringing the bill unasked is read in Spain as moving a customer on, so it does not arrive: the table is yours until you end the meal out loud. 'La cuenta, por favor' works everywhere; '¿Nos cobras?' is what people actually say at a counter. On the bill itself: 'servicio' (service) and 'cubierto' (the cover, the cutlery) are only chargeable where the regional rules permit it AND where you were told beforehand — printed on the menu or on the price list on the wall, never sprung at the end. Bread, ice and the milk in a coffee may legitimately be charged for on the same condition. What is not allowed: menu prices without VAT included, a QR code as the only way to see the menu, and refusing free tap water. And a small warning worth having: 'los servicios', in the plural, means the toilets — ask a waiter for 'el servicio' with a hopeful face and you may get directions instead of an answer.

¿Está incluido el servicio? — ehs-TAH een-kloo-EE-dhoh el sehr-BEE-thyoh — is service included?
Pagamos por separado, por favor. — pah-GAH-mohs por seh-pah-RAH-dhoh — we'll pay separately (ask BEFORE the bill is closed)
Pagamos a partes iguales. — ah PAR-tehs ee-GWAH-lehs — split it evenly, which is the easier request of the two

The tip, in one paragraph — and the screen that asks for one

Coffee or a caña at the counter: nothing, or the loose change. Neighbourhood bar or menú del día: round up, €0.50–1. Mid-range restaurant: 5–10% if you really enjoyed it, and most people leave less or nothing. Taxi: round up. Hotel porter or room cleaner: €1–2 in cash. The one role in Spain with a genuine expectation attached is the walking-tour guide, because the 'free tour' is paid in tips — that one you pay properly. The structural reason the numbers stay small is that hospitality staff are on a salary and a collective agreement: the tip is a bonus, not the wage, so leaving nothing harms nobody. The terminal offering 10/15/20% is a recent import from American practice into tourist areas, and the Spanish consumer organisation OCU stated plainly in July 2026 that a tip suggested on a receipt or a terminal is voluntary. Press 'Sin propina', or 'Otra cantidad' for your round-up. The arithmetic of what the 20% reflex costs over a fortnight — and why it does not read as generous — is a later part of its own.

Está bien así, gracias. — ehs-TAH byehn ah-SEE — 'that's fine as it is' = keep the change, and it commits you to neither tú nor usted
SIN PROPINA · OTRA CANTIDAD — 👂 the two buttons on the terminal: NO TIP · ANOTHER AMOUNT

'Ya está…' — four endings, four arguments you never have

The frame is two words and a closed list of four: pagado (paid), reservado (booked), incluido (included), hecho (done). It is closed on purpose. With an open list a learner produces something inventive at the worst possible moment; with four endings there is nothing to work out, and each one closes a specific hole. 'Ya está pagado' is the one that stops you paying twice — for the meal a friend settled, for the transfer the tour company already took, for the deposit that came off the card in April. 'Ya está reservado' stops the desk selling you what you already own. 'Ya está incluido' is for breakfast, the tourist tax and the second driver. 'Ya está hecho' is for the form you filled in online. Two related sentences worth recognising rather than producing: '¿Se lo cobro ahora?' (shall I charge you for it now?) and 'La tarjeta no ha funcionado' (the card didn't go through) — the second is nearly always the terminal, not your bank.

Ya está pagado. — yah ehs-TAH pah-GAH-dhoh — it's already been paid for
Ya está incluido. — yah ehs-TAH een-kloo-EE-dhoh — it's already included (breakfast, the tax, the second driver)

The hoja de reclamaciones: what it actually is

An official, self-copying form in triplicate: one copy for you, one for the business, one for the consumer authority of the autonomous community — the body that inspects and licenses them. It is regulated regionally, so the exact form differs from community to community, but the obligations are the same everywhere: keep them, hand them over immediately and free of charge on request, and advertise on a visible sign that they exist. Refusing to provide one is an offence in its own right, entirely separate from the merits of your complaint. We deliberately quote no fine: the amounts are set community by community, are not harmonised, and the ranges circulating in secondary legal sources are too wide to say out loud. Same for the response deadline — '10 days' circulates, but it is fixed by regional decree. The ladder is always the same three sentences in the same calm voice, sitting down: ask for the itemised bill, name the charge, ask for the form. It works well beyond restaurants: a hotel that doesn't give you the room you booked, a tour that wasn't what was advertised, a shop refusing a return, a taxi off the meter, a bar refusing free tap water. One line specifically for British listeners, which no free video will give you: since Brexit the UK is out of the European Consumer Centres network, so chasing a Spanish dispute from home is weaker for you than for any other European visitor. Sorting it out at the table, with that form in your hand, is worth more to you than it is to them.

Perdone, ¿me trae la cuenta detallada? — deh-tah-YAH-dhah — rung one: the itemised bill, and it gives everybody a graceful way out
Aquí hay un cargo que no estaba en la carta. ¿Qué es? — oon KAR-goh — rung two: name the fact, not the crime
La hoja de reclamaciones, por favor. — lah OH-khah deh rreh-klah-mah-THYOH-nehs — rung three, six words, said sitting down

Tique or factura — ask for the right piece of paper

'Un tique' is the ordinary till receipt. 'La factura' is a proper invoice carrying your name and details, and it is the document an insurer or an employer's expenses system will accept. Ask for it at the counter, while the transaction is still on their screen — reconstructing it later, by email, from another country, is a different kind of holiday. This matters most in pharmacies, clinics and hire-car counters. The long form is '¿Me puede dar la factura, por favor?', which is the same opener you already use for 'could you write it down' and 'could you tell the kitchen'; the short '¿Me da la factura?' does the same job once you are comfortable.

¿Me puede dar la factura, por favor? — meh PWEH-dheh dar lah fak-TOO-rah — the proper invoice, please
¿Me da un tique, por favor? — meh dah oon TEE-keh — just the till receipt, please
