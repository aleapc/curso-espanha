Me gusta and prefiero — two openers, one thing on the end

Both of these are learnt whole, with the thing you like sitting on the end, and neither of them has anything you need to work out at a counter. 'Me gusta mucho este barrio' — I really like this part of town. 'Prefiero el vino tinto' — I'd rather have the red. The word 'mucho' is optional and simply turns the volume up; 'me gusta este barrio' is a complete and perfectly ordinary answer. The one thing worth knowing, and it is the reason every recorded example here names exactly one thing: with two or more things on the end the opener grows an -n — 'me gustan las croquetas'. You can sidestep that for the whole fortnight by naming one thing, which is also the better answer socially, because a single named thing is what the other person can pick up and run with. The little word in front of the thing moves with it — este barrio, esta plaza, el vino, la plaza — and you take that by ear. Getting it the other way round is heard as an accent rather than an error, and the sentence lands intact either way. One warning that costs nothing to carry: 'me gusta' about a person is read as a declaration of interest rather than as friendliness. Keep it for streets, squares, plates and wine.

Me gusta mucho esta ciudad. — meh GOOS-tah MOO-choh EHS-tah thyoo-DHAHDH — I really like this city
Prefiero andar, gracias. — preh-FYEH-roh an-DAR — I'd rather walk, thanks (a whole answer to an offered taxi)

The five questions, in the order they arrive

They come in more or less this order and they are the same in a bar in Bilbao and a bakery in Cádiz. '¿De dónde eres?' — where are you from. '¿Es la primera vez?' — first time here. '¿Cuántos días estáis?' or '¿Cuánto tiempo estáis?' — how long have you got. '¿Qué te parece Madrid?' — literally 'how does Madrid strike you', and the honest short answer is the good one. '¿Y qué habéis comido?' — and what have you eaten, which is not filler: what you have eaten is treated as evidence of whether you are having a proper time. Two more you will hear in the same slot: '¿Estáis de vacaciones?' (are you on holiday) and '¿Y qué tal?' (and how is it going), which is a door held open rather than a question with an answer. The answers that work are three or four words long, and each one ends with something handed back: '¿y tú?' does it every time, and '¿eres de aquí?' — are you from here — is the one that gets you a life story. There is no politeness cost to a short answer. There is a real cost to a long one, because the person opposite is standing up, holding a glass, and expecting a turn.

¿Y tú, eres de aquí? — EH-rehs deh ah-KEE — and you, are you from here? (the one that opens everything)
¿Estáis de vacaciones? — ehs-TAIS deh bah-kah-THYOH-nehs — 👂 are you on holiday? (aimed at the two of you)

Vosotros: fifty times a day in your ear, never in your mouth

Spain has a plural 'you' that Latin America does not, and it is why half the questions in this part sound like they contain a word you have never met. Vosotros is what you get whenever somebody addresses your table rather than you: '¿qué vais a tomar?' (what are you two having), '¿habéis reservado?' (have you booked), '¿sois vosotros?' (is that you two), '¿de dónde sois?' (where are you both from), 'pasad' and 'sentaos' (come in, sit down). You are almost always on the receiving end of it — to produce one you would have to be giving instructions to two Spanish people, which is not a situation this trip contains. So it lives in your ear and not in your mouth, and if you ever genuinely need it, 'ustedes' does the job and marks you as a foreigner, which you are. Two geographical notes. In the Canaries and much of western Andalusia, 'ustedes' is used informally too, so if you spend a week in Seville and never hear 'estáis', nothing has gone wrong and nobody is being stiff with you. And the -s at the end of these forms often thins to a breath in the south — 'estáih', 'habéih' — which is the same erosion the listening part of the course deals with head on.

¿Habéis reservado? — ah-BAYS rreh-sehr-BAH-dhoh — 👂 have you booked? (at the restaurant door)
¿De dónde sois? — deh DOHN-deh soys — 👂 where are you both from?

Tú, usted, and who is doing the asking

Everything you produce in this part is the same whoever is opposite you, which is half the reason these are the sentences we chose: 'me gusta', 'prefiero', 'soy de Londres' and '¿hay algo típico de aquí?' carry no decision at all about the person you are talking to. What changes is what you hear. A man your own age on the next stool will say '¿de dónde eres?'; an older waiter in a more formal room will say '¿de dónde es usted?', and you answer both the same way. The default across bars, shops, restaurants and taxis is tú, and it is the opposite of what most English speakers assume — reaching for usted everywhere is not more polite, it just marks you as somebody working from a phrasebook, and you may get a cheerful 'no me trates de usted, que me haces mayor' for it. Save usted for three places: police and officials, somebody clearly elderly, and a formal counter. If somebody uses tú with you, use it back. If you hand your question back with '¿y tú?' to a man of seventy, nothing breaks; the worst case is that he finds it friendly.

¿De dónde es usted? — deh DOHN-deh es oos-TEDH — 👂 the usted version of the first question
¿Y usted? — ee oos-TEDH — 👂 and you? (the version you will hear back from an older stranger)

The sixth question, and the one subject you leave alone

'¿Hay algo típico de aquí?' is the single most profitable sentence in this part, and it works because it gives the other person a job they enjoy: telling a visitor what is worth knowing about their own town. Ask it once, then stop talking. What comes back is regularly a dish that is not on the board, a bar with no sign on the front, the one day of the week the market is any good, and — often enough that it stops being a coincidence — an instruction to the barman about what you are drinking next. Two neighbours of it: '¿qué me recomienda?' is the older, wider version and the answer is usually too long to follow, and '¿cuál me recomienda, este o aquel?' is the two-way version from the set-lunch part, which is easier to catch because the answer is one of two things you can already see. What stays out of the first five minutes with somebody you have just met is the political history: Franco, the monarchy, the Civil War, Basque and Catalan independence. There is no legal risk anywhere in that list — Spain argues about all of it openly and constantly — the risk is purely social, and it is the same instinct that stops you asking a stranger in Belfast which side his family was on. If he opens it himself, listen and ask rather than rule: 'no conozco bien el tema, cuéntame' hands it back honestly. Do not guess which side anybody is on from the city, the accent or the language they are speaking; the mental map that says 'speaks Catalan, therefore pro-independence' is wrong often enough to be noticed on the spot. And football is politics carried on by other means: saying you support both of them is the only answer worse than picking one. The full picture is a part of its own in the last module.

¿Hay algo típico de aquí? — ai AL-goh TEE-pee-koh deh ah-KEE — is there something they only do here?
No conozco bien el tema, cuéntame. — noh koh-NOTH-koh byen el TEH-mah, KWEN-tah-meh — I don't know much about it, tell me
