Cuál, and why a two-way question beats an open one

'¿Qué me recomienda?' is the question everybody teaches and almost nobody can survive the answer to: it is open, and what comes back is a sentence at full speed with four nouns in it that you have never heard. '¿Cuál me llevo, este o aquel?' does the same social work — you are still handing the decision to the person who knows — but the answer can only be one of two things you are already looking at, and it will arrive as 'este' with a finger. That is the whole trick, and it is the same trick as '¿Hay…?' from part eight: make the reply fit in your head. Cuál is 'which one' when you are choosing between things already in view; qué is 'what' when nothing has been put in front of you yet. You will hear both and you only need one. Note that you never have to name either object: 'este' and 'aquel' — this one, that one over there — plus a finger, will do it at a cheese counter, a fishmonger's, a wine shelf and a bakery.

¿Cuál me llevo, este o aquel? — KWAHL meh YEH-boh — which do I take, this one or that one?
¿Cuál está mejor hoy? — KWAHL ehs-TAH meh-KHOR oy — which is best today? (when there are more than two and you would rather she decided)

Me gusta and prefiero — the front that never moves

Both of these are learnt whole, with the thing you like hung off the end, and you never take the front of either apart. 'Me gusta' plus a thing — me gusta este barrio, me gusta el vino tinto, me gusta mucho. 'Prefiero' plus a thing or plus an action — prefiero andar, prefiero el tinto, prefiero sentarme fuera. There is an old exam question about which word in 'me gusta' is doing what, and it is genuinely of no use to you at a table: the recorded version is the version, and it has never let anybody down in a square. Two things worth knowing. First, 'me gusta mucho' is warmer than 'me gusta' and costs one word, so spend it. Second, when they ask you in the plural — '¿os gusta?' — they are asking the two of you, and the answer is still 'me gusta' or 'nos gusta' and either is fine. The neighbouring one you will hear all evening and never need to produce is '¿qué te parece?' — what do you make of it — and 'me gusta mucho' is a complete answer to that too.

Prefiero sentarme fuera. — preh-FYEH-roh sen-TAR-meh FWEH-rah — I'd rather sit outside
Nos gusta mucho. — nohs GOOS-tah MOO-choh — we like it a lot (when the question came at both of you)

Se puede — the sentence with nobody in it

You will hear 'aquí no se puede' — you can't, here — more than almost anything else on this trip, on a square at midnight, at a museum door, in a chapel with a phone in your hand. It is worth recognising instantly and it is worth understanding why it sounds blunt and is not: there is no 'you' in it at all. It does not say you are not allowed; it says the thing is not done here. That is why it comes out flat and why nobody is being told off. The question form, '¿se puede beber aquí?', is the least confrontational question in the language for exactly the same reason, and if you want it, it is yours — but we have deliberately not drilled it, because '¿Puedo…?' from part six already gets you the same answer and you have already paid for it. Where 'se puede' earns its keep is on the receiving end: hear it, put the thing down or walk on, and there is nothing else to say.

Aquí no se puede. — ah-KEE noh seh PWEH-dheh — 👂 you can't, here. Not a telling-off; a statement about the place
¿Se puede pasar? — seh PWEH-dheh pah-SAR — 👂 may one come through? Said at a doorway, and to nobody in particular

Who actually writes these rules, and where to look before you go

The single most useful thing in this part is not a number, it is the shape: the street rules that carry an amount are municipal ordinances, written by each ayuntamiento, and there is no national table of them. That is why the same shirtless walk is €120 to €300 in Barcelona under the civility ordinance in force since February 2026, €100 to €200 across the whole of Palma de Mallorca, and nothing at all in a village forty kilometres inland. The exceptions that are national are few and worth knowing by name: drinking in the street sits in the 2015 public security act at €100 to €600, and the taxi you take without a licence can cost the passenger up to €600 under the same sort of provision — that one comes from the UK Foreign Office's own advice for Spain. What changed in 2026, and what makes the old advice that 'they will never chase you for it' out of date, is collection: the Barcelona ordinance requires a non-resident to pay on the spot and the Guàrdia Urbana carry card readers. If you want the actual text before you travel, the search that works is the name of the town plus 'ordenanza de civismo' or 'ordenanza de convivencia' — every ayuntamiento publishes its own, in full, free.

¿De cuánto es la multa? — deh KWAN-toh es lah MOOL-tah — how much is it? Ask before anything is tapped
¿Me lo puede dar por escrito, por favor? — por ehs-KREE-toh — could you give me that in writing? The receipt is your only proof you paid

The car, the sticker and the camera

The low-emission zone is the one rule in this part that is enforced without a human being anywhere near it, and it is the reason the afternoon starts with where you leave the car. Since 1 January 2026, every Spanish municipality over fifty thousand inhabitants has had to operate one, and they sit exactly where the old town is. A camera reads the plate; the penalty is a serious infraction at up to €200, halved to €100 if you pay within twenty days of the notice. The structural trap is the sticker: the DGT environmental badge is issued only to vehicles registered in Spain, so a British car does not have one and cannot simply buy one at the border — several cities run a foreign-vehicle register instead, which you complete online, city by city, before you drive in. A hire car is not automatically safe either: it carries a Spanish plate and a badge, but you still need to know which category it is in, and if a fine does arrive the hire company passes it to you with an administrative fee attached. The practical rule survives all of that unchanged: see the letters, park outside, walk in. Historic centres are small and walking them was the plan.

¿Este coche tiene etiqueta ambiental? ¿Cuál? — eh-tee-KEH-tah am-byen-TAL — does this car have an emissions badge, and which one? Asked at the hire desk, not at the barrier
¿Dónde puedo aparcar sin entrar? — seen en-TRAR — where can I park without going in?

The vape, with a date written on it

This is the most perishable thing in the whole course and it is being told to you with its date attached on purpose. As of July 2026 the position is: smoking indoors has been illegal in Spain since 2011, and smoking or vaping on a bar or restaurant terrace is still legal. On 21 July 2026 the cabinet approved a draft bill that would ban both on terraces, on beaches, at public pools, in sports grounds and national parks, would create fifteen-metre protection zones around hospitals, schools and playgrounds, and would for the first time treat an electronic cigarette, a nicotine pouch, a water pipe and heated tobacco exactly like a cigarette, whether or not there is nicotine in them. It is a draft, it is in a fragmented parliament, and nobody can honestly tell you today what month it lands. So the honest instruction is the one in the audio: if you are travelling after this, ask at the bar before you light anything, and take the answer from the person who would be fined rather than from us. This entry is on the review list for every edition of the course.

¿Se puede fumar aquí? — seh PWEH-dheh foo-MAR — is smoking allowed here? The one question that settles it, asked of the bar
¿Puedo vapear en la terraza? — bah-peh-AR — can I vape on the terrace? Same answer, same person
