Ponme, me pones, me pone — three doors, and why we recorded two

All three fetch the same beer. '¿Me pones…?' is the one you learned at the counter in part eleven and it is the safest thing in your mouth: it sounds like a request, it works with anybody, and nobody has ever been marked down for it. 'Ponme…' is the short one you will hear all around you — it drops the question and puts the verb first, which is why it sounds abrupt written down in English and does not sound abrupt at all in the room. The politeness is carried by 'por favor' on the end and by your tone, not by anything on the front. '¿Me pone…?' is the third, and it is simply the version you use with somebody older or in a smarter place; you will hear it and you never have to choose it under pressure, because the other two cover the fortnight. What we have deliberately not done is give you a table of when to use which. There isn't a decision to make: pick the one that arrives first, put a please on it, and get on with your evening. The one thing worth knowing is that all three take exactly the same tail — a drink, a plate, two of something, or 'lo mismo que él' when you cannot name any of it.

¿Me pones un cortado? — meh POH-nehs oon kor-TAH-dhoh — a small coffee with a splash of milk, please
Ponme otra, por favor. — POHN-meh OH-trah — put me another one, please (the two short frames stacked together)

The order that is not a queue — and the counters where it is

At a busy bar there is no line and there is an order, and the two facts sit together perfectly well. The barman holds a rough sequence of faces in his head, refreshed every time he comes down the row and somebody's eyes come up to meet his. What breaks it for a visitor is not rudeness, it is geometry: he cannot include a face that is a metre and a half behind the counter, half turned away, waiting to be invited. Two steps and a hand on the wood put you in it, and holding his eye for the second and a half he offers you is what keeps you there. If two of you are ordering, one of you does the ordering — a huddle facing each other reads as a table that has not decided. And there is a proper exception, which is the whole reason it is worth learning as a habit rather than a rule: in pharmacies, bakeries, butchers, fish counters and most places with a ticket machine, the order is kept out loud. You walk in, you say '¿Quién es el último?', you memorise the person who answers, and you go after them. That sentence gets its own turn in part seventeen. Walking to the front of one of those is the one version of this that genuinely annoys people.

¿Quién es el último? — kyen es el OOL-tee-moh — 👂 who is last? — the spoken queue, at a pharmacy or a bakery
¿Qué te pongo? — keh teh POHN-goh — 👂 what am I putting you? — your second and a half, being handed over

Reaching a waiter across a room

Two words do all of it. '¡Perdone!' is the polite general-purpose one and works at any distance and any price level. '¡Oiga!' is literally 'listen' and it is the one that carries; it sounds sharp translated and is entirely ordinary in a busy room. Either one goes with a hand raised to about shoulder height, said once, at the volume the room is already running at — which is louder than a British dining room and is not an argument. Then you stop and let him finish what he is carrying. What does not work, in any bar or restaurant in the country, at any price: clicking your fingers, whistling, calling '¡chico!' or waving a note in the air. Those are not regional or generational; they read the same everywhere. In a quiet dining room the hand alone usually does it, and in the very smartest ones you will notice nobody says anything at all. One more thing worth having: nobody is going to come over and ask you how everything is tasting. That silence is not neglect. It is the same instinct that keeps the bill off your table — your table is treated as yours, and being interrupted in it is what would be rude.

¡Perdone! — pehr-DOH-neh — excuse me (the general-purpose call, any distance, any place)
Perdone, ¿nos cobras? — pehr-DOH-neh, nohs KOH-brahs — excuse me, will you take ours? (the local-sounding way to ask for the bill)

Why the padding costs you, and what it is doing

British and Spanish courtesy are both doing polite work; they are simply pointed at different things. English courtesy is built on staying out of somebody's way — hedges, apologies and questions wrapped around a request so it never lands as an imposition: 'sorry, I was wondering whether I could possibly get a beer at some point, if that's alright?'. Spanish courtesy is built on closeness — you show respect by treating the other person as somebody you are already at ease with, which means short, direct and warm, with the politeness carried by tone, by 'por favor' and 'gracias' at the ends rather than the middles, and by the fact that you greeted them at all. Linguists describe the two poles as negative and positive politeness, and neither is more polite than the other; they simply misread across the gap. Translated into a bar, the practical effect is one-directional and unlucky for you: the hedged version takes six seconds longer to arrive at its point, and six seconds is roughly four times the attention you have been given. He does not decide you are rude. He decides you have not decided, and he moves along the row. Drop the run-up, keep the please, and the whole thing works.

Hola. Ponme una caña, por favor. — the entire transaction, front to back — greeting, order, please
Perdone, ¿me pone otra? — pehr-DOH-neh, meh POH-neh OH-trah — excuse me, another one? (the same thing in a smarter room)

Caña, clara, pincho — the tails you can hang on it tonight

A 'caña' is the small glass of draught you saw in part eleven, and it stays the default all fortnight. A 'clara' is that same beer with lemonade in it, and in summer grown adults order it all day long. In some places you will be asked which one you want: 'con limón' is the lemonade version, and 'con casera' is made with plain fizzy water instead, so it is the less sweet of the two. A 'pincho' is a small portion, usually on a slice of bread, and 'un pincho de tortilla' — a wedge of the potato omelette sitting under the glass dome — is the single most reliable thing to eat standing up in Spain. In the Basque Country the same word is spelled 'pintxo', the plates sit out on the counter for you to help yourself, and the cocktail sticks left on your plate are what gets added up at the end, so you keep every one of them — that half of it is covered in part eleven, along with where the free plate is a habit and where it is an item. And when nothing on the counter has a name you know, the sentence that never fails is '¿me pones lo mismo que él?' with your chin pointed at the man beside you, who will be delighted.

¿Me pones una clara con limón? — meh POH-nehs OO-nah KLAH-rah kon lee-MOHN — a shandy with lemonade, please
¿Me pones dos pinchos de tortilla? — meh POH-nehs dohs PEEN-chohs deh tor-TEE-yah — two wedges of potato omelette, please
