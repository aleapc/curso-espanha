Cash machines: why the fee decides how often, not how much

A withdrawal in Spain typically costs €3–5, with the reported spread running from about €0.50 at Bankinter to €6 at BBVA, and the free-standing Euronet machines add €1.95–4.99 of their own on top of anything your bank charges. Because the fee is per withdrawal and not per euro, three small withdrawals cost you roughly €6–10 more than one larger one. The practical rule: use a street machine belonging to an actual bank, take out more and less often, and skip the machine in the arrivals hall — it is the most expensive one you will meet all trip. Separately, if the screen ever offers to charge you in pounds or dollars, that is the dynamic currency conversion markup, and it is dealt with in the part on the bill.

¿Dónde hay un cajero? — DOHN-deh ai oon kah-KHEH-roh — where's a cash machine?
¿Hay un cajero por aquí? — the yes/no version, when you only need to know whether to keep walking

Card, cash, and the fifty-euro note

Contactless is the default in Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia, and most urban businesses have dropped the old €5 minimum. But there is no legal obligation in Spain to accept cards: a bar can lawfully run on cash alone, and the sign that says so is SOLO EFECTIVO. Cash still dominates in traditional bakeries, small pharmacies, family guesthouses, small family tapas bars in Andalusia, fruit and craft stalls and street markets. Carry €40–60 per person in small notes. The problem is never the total, it is the shape of it: fifty-euro notes are routinely refused in small shops, and a bar selling a €3.20 coffee genuinely cannot break one at nine in the morning.

¿Puedo pagar con tarjeta? — PWEH-dhoh pah-GAR kon tar-KHEH-tah — can I pay by card?
¿Aceptan tarjeta o solo efectivo? — ah-THEP-tan tar-KHEH-tah oh SOH-loh eh-fek-TEE-boh — card, or cash only?

The phone: what to do at home and what to do on the ground

A travel eSIM needs an internet connection to activate, the QR code arrives by email, and you will land without either — so it is installed and tested at home, on your own wifi, which is why it lives on the pre-flight list rather than in this hour. On the ground, two sentences cover most of it: '¿Hay wifi?' and '¿Cuál es la contraseña?'. On a receipt the password is often printed as 'clave' or 'contraseña'. If you land with nothing at all, a Spanish prepaid SIM has to be registered against an identity document at the point of sale and the physical passport is what they want in their hand — a photo of it on your screen is routinely refused, with Vodafone and Movistar counters reported as the strictest.

¿Hay wifi? ¿Cuál es la contraseña? — ai WEE-fee · lah kon-trah-SEH-nyah — is there wifi? what's the password?
Quiero una tarjeta SIM de prepago, solo datos. — prepaid SIM, data only — and have the passport out before you ask

Why 'puedo' and not something more polite

'¿Puedo…?' is first person. It asks about you, which means it commits you to nothing about the person opposite — no choice between 'tú' and 'usted' to make under pressure at a counter, which is the decision English speakers reliably get backwards. The English instinct is that formal equals polite and polite equals safe; in most tourist scenes in Spain the formal register simply reads as distance, and the padding ('I was wondering if I could possibly…') is what slows you down. The politeness lives in your tone and in a 'por favor' at the end, not in five layers of hedging. The one place to keep the formal register and the queue discipline is an official window: police, Guardia Civil, government counters.

Perdone, ¿puedo dejar la maleta aquí? — the full move: get their attention, then ask in six words
¿Puedo entrar con esto? — PWEH-dhoh en-TRAR kon EHS-toh — the version for anything you have no word for
