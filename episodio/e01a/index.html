Why the words you already recognise are the ones nobody understands — and the hand that fixes it

Guide

Welcome to Dime. I'm Alice, and I'm going to be honest with you from the first minute: Spanish pronunciation is not hard. It's just different in five small ways, and English speakers get all five wrong at once. Here's the strange part — the word you'll mangle worst is the word you already know. 'Hospital'. 'Chocolate'. 'Gracias'. You recognise them, so you don't slow down, and out comes something a Spanish barman genuinely cannot parse. So here's our deal, and it's a modest one: the right vowels in the fifty phrases that get you through the trip. Not sounding Spanish by September. Now put your hand flat on your leg, palm down. It's about to become your teacher. Spanish has five vowels — five, not twelve — and each one is a single, flat, unmoving sound. Ah. Eh. Ee. Oh. Oo. English vowels slide: 'no' comes out 'nooow', 'café' comes out 'ca-fay'. Spanish ones don't move. Keep the palm flat while you say each one — if your hand tilts, your vowel slid.

The pronunciation respelling used in this app

Everything written in the grey line under the Spanish is aimed at an English-reading eye. ah, eh, ee, oh, oo are the five vowels — as in father, bed, see (short), or (without the r), boot. 'th' is the C before e/i and the Z — as in think, never as in this. 'dh' is the softened D between vowels — as in mother. 'kh' is the J and the G before e/i — as in Scottish loch. 'ny' is the Ñ — as in canyon. 'y' is LL and Y — as in yes, never the 'zh' of measure (that's Buenos Aires, not Madrid). 'r' is one tap; 'rr' is two or three. B and V are the same sound in Spain — there is no V. H is always silent. CAPITALS mark the stressed syllable.

gracias — GRAH-thyahs — the C is a 'th', the final S is an S
jamón — khah-MOHN — the loch, then the punch on the last syllable

The stress rule, in full

1. Word ends in a vowel, N or S → stress the second-to-last syllable: CA-sa, CO-men, gra-CIAS. 2. Word ends in any other consonant → stress the last syllable: hos-pi-TAL, ciu-DAD, es-pa-ÑOL. 3. There's a written accent → the accent overrides both rules, always: te-LÉ-fo-no, po-li-CÍ-a, ca-FÉ. The accent mark also separates pairs that mean very different things, and this is where it costs you something real.

papá / papa — dad / potato
está / esta — is / this
sí / si — yes / if

Two more free gifts: Ñ and the soft D

The Ñ is not two sounds, it's one, and you already own it: it's the middle of 'onion'. In fact 'canyon' is the Spanish 'cañón', borrowed — that's etymology, not analogy. Say España in three beats with the Ñ inside the middle beat. Separately: a D between two vowels goes soft, all the way to the 'th' of 'father'. So 'nada' is closer to NAH-tha than NAH-da, and 'Madrid' ends in a whisper. And remember there is no V in Spain: 'vino' starts with a B.

España — ehs-PAH-nyah — three beats, one Ñ
No pasa nada. — noh PAH-sah NAH-dhah — 'no harm done', the most useful throwaway line in Spain

The rhythm argument, for the curious

The classic description separates syllable-timed languages (Spanish) from stress-timed ones (English). Modern phonetics argues about how strictly that holds and prefers measuring variability instead. None of that matters to you in a bar. What matters is the behaviour: in Spanish every syllable gets paid in full, and English speakers habitually short-change the unstressed ones into a schwa. Tap them all the same size and you have bought yourself the single biggest gain available.

The quiet false friends (read, don't drill)

These are the ones that cost you dignity rather than money, so they live here in text rather than in your ear. 'Largo' is long, not large. 'Ropa' is clothes, not rope. 'Carpeta' is a folder, not a carpet. 'Pie' is a foot. 'Sensible' means sensitive. 'Actualmente' means currently. 'Red' is a network — 'la red wifi'. 'Introducir' means to insert, which is why the cash machine tells you to 'introduzca su tarjeta'. And 'envolver' means to gift-wrap, so when the shop assistant asks '¿se lo envuelvo?', she is not involving you in anything.

Introduzca su tarjeta. — Insert your card. (cash machine)
¿Se lo envuelvo? — Shall I wrap it for you? (shop)
