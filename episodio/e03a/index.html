Why the terrace costs more — and when it stops being legal

Spanish consumer law lets a bar charge different amounts for the counter, the indoor tables and the terrace. What it does not let them do is spring it on you: the price list has to be displayed where a customer can see it, and the amounts shown must already include tax. So a terrace supplement that appears on the posted list is legal and there is nothing to argue about. One that appears only on the bill is a different matter, and that is what the complaint form later in the course is built for. The gap per drink is small; it is the fortnight, three drinks a day and two of you that makes it stop being small. We have deliberately not put a number on it — there is no published series for it, only anecdote.

¿Es el mismo precio en la terraza? — es el MEES-moh PREH-thyoh en lah teh-RRAH-thah — Is it the same price on the terrace?
¿Me deja ver la carta en papel, por favor? — meh DEH-khah behr lah KAR-tah en pah-PEL — Could I see a paper menu, please? (a QR code cannot be the only menu)

'Caña' and its relatives

A 'caña' is a small glass of draught beer, served cold and drunk fast, which is the entire point. Order 'a beer' in English and you get a bottle, which costs more and goes warm. Other sizes on the board: a 'doble' is twice a caña; a 'tercio' is a 330ml bottle; and a 'jarra' of beer is a big glass, while a 'jarra de agua' is the jug for the middle of the table — same word, two objects, and the one after it tells you which. For wine, 'tinto' is red, 'blanco' white, 'rosado' rosé, and in some regions a small glass of red is a 'chato' or a 'corto'. A 'clara' is beer with lemonade, and it is not embarrassing in August. All of them slot into the same frame: ¿Me pones un tercio? ¿Me pones una clara? Nothing else in the sentence moves.

¿Me pones un tercio, por favor? — meh POH-nehs oon TEHR-thyoh — Could I have a bottle of beer, please?
Otra ronda, por favor. — OH-trah RROHN-dah — Another round, please. (the second frame, with the round on the end)

Free water, paid bread, and the free tapa that is only free in some places

The water is the cleanest right you have on this trip: since Ley 7/2022 came into force, hospitality venues must always offer the possibility of drinking unpackaged water free of charge. It was written as an anti-plastic measure rather than a consumer one, but the effect is identical. Ask for a 'jarra' rather than a glass — same law, enough for the table, one trip for the waiter. Bread, ice and the milk in your coffee are all things a bar may legitimately charge for, provided you were told beforehand or it is on the posted list; the offence is almost never the charge itself, it is the surprise. Which is why 'is it on the list?' is a more useful question than 'is this allowed?'. And the free tapa is geography, not law: eastern Andalusia — Granada above all, where a different plate comes with every round — plus Almería, where you pick from a list, and parts of Castilla y León and Galicia. Barcelona, Madrid, San Sebastián and Valencia treat the plate as an item you are buying. The Basque Country runs a third system: pintxos sit out on the counter, you help yourself, and the cocktail sticks left on your plate are what gets added up, so do not tidy them away.

Una jarra de agua del grifo, por favor. — OO-nah KHAH-rrah deh AH-gwah del GREE-foh — A jug of tap water, please.
¿Cuántos palillos tienes? — KWAN-tohs pah-LEE-yohs TYEH-nehs — 👂 'How many sticks have you got?' — he is not accusing you of anything, he is adding up.

The clock, in one paragraph

Typically: breakfast is coffee and toast between half seven and nine; a second coffee around eleven; the main meal of the day, 'la comida', runs from two until about half three; a snack, 'la merienda', around five or six; and dinner, 'la cena', from nine, with kitchens taking last orders around half eleven. None of this is a rule and none of it is official — it is what most of the country happens to do, and the full version of it is a part of its own later in the course. The practical consequence is the one that matters now: a place happily serving full dinners at seven in the evening is, almost by definition, a place that lives on visitors, and that is where the inflated bill and the price-free 'tourist menu' live too. Eating at the local hour is not authenticity theatre. It is a filter.

¿A qué hora abre la cocina? — ah keh OH-rah AH-breh lah koh-THEE-nah — What time does the kitchen open?
