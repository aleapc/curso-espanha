The day, hour by hour

Typical, not official — no Spanish institution publishes a national dinner time, so treat this as the shape of the day rather than a timetable pinned up at the border. Desayuno 07:30 to 09:00, and it is small: coffee and a toast with tomato and oil. A second coffee or a snack around 11:00, which is the one most visitors never notice and the one that fixes an early start. La comida, the main meal of the day, 14:00 to 15:30 or 16:00 — this is the big one, and it is where the menú del día lives. La merienda 17:00 to 18:30, coffee and something sweet. La cena from 21:00, with kitchens reopening around 20:00 to 20:30 and last orders somewhere near 23:30. Turning up between 18:00 and 19:00 gets you one of three answers: closed, open but drinks only, or open and empty — and open and empty at that hour is usually a house that survives on people who do not know the hours.

¿A qué hora abre la cocina? — ah keh OH-rah AH-breh lah koh-THEE-nah — what time does the kitchen open? (the question that plans the evening)
¿Hasta qué hora sirven? — AHS-tah keh OH-rah SEER-behn — until what time do you serve? (the other end of the same clock)

Why nine o'clock is not as late as it sounds

Mainland Spain sits over roughly the same line of longitude as London, and by the sun it belongs in the same hour as Britain. It does not run on that hour. In 1940 the clocks were moved forward to central European time and they were never moved back, which puts the whole country an hour ahead of its own daylight — two, in summer. A parliamentary commission recommended returning to the old zone in 2013 and nothing came of it. So when a table sits down to dinner at nine, the sun is roughly where a seven or eight o'clock sun would be at home, depending on the season, and lunch at half two is a one o'clock lunch by the light coming through the window. This is worth knowing for exactly one reason: it stops the schedule feeling like a national quirk you are being asked to accommodate, and turns it into arithmetic you can plan around. It is also why the light lasts so far into a summer evening.

Las nueve de la noche. — lahs NWEH-beh deh lah NOH-cheh — nine at night, which by the sun is nearer eight

La sobremesa — the hour nobody bills you for

The plates go and nothing happens. No bill, no hovering, no one refilling your water pointedly. That gap has a name — la sobremesa, literally 'over the table' — and it is not the end of the meal, it is part of it: coffee, maybe something small and strong afterwards, and a conversation that is allowed to run. Two consequences for you. First, the bill genuinely will not come until you ask, which you already know from part fourteen; the waiter is not neglecting you, he is leaving you alone on purpose. Second, and this is the one that changes an afternoon: the table is yours for as long as you want it, and one more coffee is the whole rent. A Sunday lunch can still be at the table when the light changes. The counter-case is the one to watch for: if the bill lands on the cloth without you asking, that is a place letting the table twice, and you are being moved along politely. It happens on tourist strips and almost nowhere else.

Otro café, por favor. — OH-troh kah-FEH — another coffee, please (the rent on another hour of the table)
No tenemos prisa. — noh teh-NEH-mohs PREE-sah — 👂 we're in no hurry (what the next table says, and what the room assumes)

Booking, and telling the hour back

Restaurant booking runs on 'para las' plus the hour: para las nueve, para las diez. Half past is 'y media', quarter past 'y cuarto', quarter to 'menos cuarto' — so half past nine is 'las nueve y media' and quarter to ten is 'las diez menos cuarto'. Booking at half nine marks you instantly as somebody who did the reading, and in a busy place it is the sitting that goes first; ten o'clock is the one still going at eight in the evening. Two answers come straight back and you only ever have to recognise them: '¿para cuántas personas?' — for how many — and '¿a nombre de quién?' — under what name. Both are answered in two words. And for a phone booking, which you will occasionally have to make from a hotel desk, the same sentence works exactly as it does at the door: nothing about it changes on a telephone.

Quería una mesa para las diez. — keh-REE-ah OO-nah MEH-sah PAH-rah lahs DYETH — I'd like a table for ten
¿A nombre de quién? — ah NOM-breh deh kyen — 👂 under what name? (answer with your surname, spelled slowly)
