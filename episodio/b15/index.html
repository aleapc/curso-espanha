Póngame, quería, and which one to reach for

Both open a counter and both are already conjugated for you, so nothing has to be worked out on the spot. 'Póngame…' is the counter verb proper — literally 'put me' — and it is what you hear at a market stall, a bakery, a deli counter and a fishmonger. It expects a quantity: medio kilo, un kilo, cien gramos, dos barras. 'Quería…' is the softer, wider one you already own from part twelve: it takes a thing rather than a weight, and it works in a ticket office, a chemist, a hotel and a shop as well as at a counter. Where they overlap, use whichever comes out first — nobody is grading you, and a stall holder who hears either will start filling a bag. What closes the order is the part people forget: 'Nada más, gracias.' Without it the offers keep arriving, because as far as she is concerned you have not finished.

Póngame dos barras de pan, por favor. — POHN-gah-meh dohs BAH-rrahs deh pan — two loaves, please
Quería medio kilo de esto. — keh-REE-ah MEH-dhyoh KEE-loh deh EHS-toh — half a kilo of this (with a finger doing the naming)

Talla, número, and the two words for change

'Talla' is a garment size and 'número' is a shoe size: ask a shoe shop for a bigger 'talla' and you will be shown a jumper, which is a harmless mistake and a good story. The pair worth separating properly is 'cambiar' and 'cambio'. 'Cambiar' is to swap one thing for another — 'Se puede cambiar' is what you ask about the shirt. 'El cambio' is money coming back to you, which is why '¿Tiene cambio de cincuenta?' asks whether she can break a big note, and 'Quédese con el cambio' tells a taxi driver to keep it. Same root, two errands, and the sentence around it tells you which one is in play.

¿Qué número usa? — keh NOO-meh-roh OO-sah — 👂 what shoe size are you? — the question that comes back at you in a shoe shop
¿Dónde están los probadores? — DOHN-deh ehs-TAN lohs proh-bah-DOH-rehs — where are the fitting rooms?

The tique, and what a shop actually owes you

Keep the receipt. A Spanish shop is not obliged to take back something you have simply gone off, so a change of mind depends on that shop's own policy — many do accept returns, usually within a stated number of days, and almost all of them ask for the tique before anything else. That is why the question goes in before the card comes out: '¿Se puede cambiar o devolver? ¿En cuántos días?'. This is a different matter from something that arrives broken or stops working, which is covered by the legal guarantee of conformity that every seller in Spain owes under the consumer act (Real Decreto Legislativo 1/2007, as amended in 2021); that case is not a favour and does not depend on the shop's goodwill. Sale items are not a separate universe either: the same guarantee covers a garment bought in the rebajas and one bought in May.

¿Se puede cambiar o devolver? ¿En cuántos días? — seh PWEH-dheh kam-BYAR oh deh-bol-BEHR — can it be exchanged or returned? within how many days?
¿Me da el tique, por favor? — meh dah el TEE-keh — can I have the receipt, please? ('la factura' is the version with your details on it, which is the one an insurer wants)

Why the fruit is not yours to pick

At a traditional market stall the seller chooses, weighs and bags, and there is usually a sign about it: NO TOCAR LA FRUTA. It reads as officious from the outside and it is nothing of the kind — she is managing stock that has to be sold in a particular order, and handling by strangers costs her the ripe end of the box. Stand back, point at the tray, name the weight, and she will pick better than you would. In a supermarket the arrangement inverts completely: the gloves are on a roll beside the produce, you pick and bag your own, and standing there waiting to be served is the thing that looks out of place.

NO TOCAR LA FRUTA — noh toh-KAR lah FROO-tah — 👂 do not touch the fruit
Póngame un kilo de naranjas, por favor. — POHN-gah-meh oon KEE-loh deh nah-RAHN-khahs — a kilo of oranges, please
