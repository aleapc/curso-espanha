Who speaks what, and where you will meet it

Castilian Spanish — the one this course teaches — is official across the whole territory, and it is what you will be answered in the moment anybody hears your accent. Alongside it, Catalan is co-official in Catalonia, the Balearics and the Valencian Community; Basque (euskara) in the Basque Country and the north of Navarre; Galician (galego) in Galicia. Catalan and Galician are Romance languages, so a lot of written Catalan and most written Galician is half-guessable if you already read Spanish. Basque is unrelated to any known living language, which is why nothing in it looks familiar and why you should stop trying to find a root in it. The practical shape of this for a visitor is lopsided and worth saying plainly: the co-official language matters to you almost entirely in writing — on doors, boards, timetables and menus — and almost not at all in speech, because the speech comes back to you in Castilian.

Bon dia · Bona tarda · Si us plau · Moltes gràcies · Adéu — Catalan: good morning · good afternoon · please · thank you very much · bye
Kaixo · Eskerrik asko / Bo día · Grazas — Basque: hello · thank you / Galician: good morning · thanks

The sign module, and the two that earn their keep

Recognition only — nothing here is ever produced out loud, and the return on being able to write a Catalan sign is zero. Catalan: obert / tancat (open / closed) · sortida (exit) · entrada · lavabos or serveis (toilets) · estirar / empènyer (pull / push) · esmorzar / dinar / sopar (breakfast / lunch / dinner, which is how you read opening hours) · preu (price) · pa (bread) · aigua (water). Basque: irteera (exit) · komuna (toilet) · itxita (closed). Galician: saída (exit) · aberto / pechado (open / closed). Two of these do the work of all the rest: tancat, because otherwise you stand outside a shut door reading it as an invitation, and sortida, because otherwise you walk past your own way out. One pair worth naming, because it is the one people brace for and it turns out to be safe: estirar is PULL in Catalan, and the Spanish tirar you learned in part ten is PULL as well, so the two agree. The one that gives you nothing is empènyer, which is push and looks like no word you own.

TANCAT PER VACANCES — 👂 closed for the holidays — the Catalan of CERRADO POR VACACIONES, and you will meet it all August
ITXITA — 👂 closed, in Basque. If the door does not move, this is usually why

Why bon dia works when it is not needed

Nobody in Barcelona needs you to speak Catalan and practically nobody expects it. That is precisely why two words of it land: the greeting is read as a courtesy rather than as a claim, and it costs the person opposite nothing to accept. What normally follows is that they switch to Castilian immediately, and beginners read that switch as a rejection. It is not one. Greeting in the language of the house and then serving you in the language you both have are two different courtesies performed by the same person in the same second. Take the switch, keep going in your ordinary Spanish, and put adéu on the way out — greeting on the way in and on the way out is Spanish habit anyway, and doing both in Catalan is a two-word investment for the whole trip. The one thing that reliably goes the other way is the sentence 'it's basically Spanish, isn't it'. Catalan has its own grammar and orthography and about a thousand years of literature behind it, and it has been co-official since article 3 of the 1978 constitution. Asking a question about the word on the board gets you the opposite reaction to making an observation about the language.

Bon dia! Digui. — 👂 morning, go ahead — digui is the Catalan of dime, and it is your cue to order
De res. — 👂 not at all — the Catalan de nada, and the usual answer to moltes gràcies

Valencian, and the mistake pointed the other way

In the Valencian Community the same language is officially called valencià, and that is the name on the menus, the street signs and the official paperwork there. Visitors who have just learned that Catalan is a language of its own sometimes go one step too far and correct a Valencian — 'that's Catalan, isn't it' — which lands exactly as badly as calling Catalan a dialect, and for the same reason: it files somebody's own name for their own language under a name chosen elsewhere. The rule that costs you nothing and never misfires is to use the name that is printed on the sign in front of you. The linguistic question is genuinely disputed by people who care a great deal about it, it is not a question a visitor is required to have a view on, and it belongs in the same drawer as the subject in part thirty-two.

Bon dia · Bona vesprada — Valencia: the afternoon greeting is vesprada there, where Barcelona says tarda

Vosotros: in your ear, not your mouth

Peninsular Spanish has a second-person plural that Latin American Spanish does not, and it is the form that will be aimed at you and your travelling companion roughly fifty times a day: ¿qué vais a tomar?, ¿habéis reservado?, ¿de dónde sois?, ¿sois vosotros?, pasad, sentaos, tenéis que esperar. You are almost always the target of it and almost never the speaker, because to need to produce it you would have to be giving instructions to two Spaniards at once. So it lives in the receptive half of this course on purpose. If you ever do need it, ustedes is grammatical, is understood everywhere, and marks you as a foreigner — which you are, and which costs nothing. Two useful edges: in the Canaries and much of western Andalusia ustedes is used for friends as well, so if you never hear vosotros in Seville, nothing has gone wrong; and the endings themselves are the giveaway to listen for — the -áis and -éis at the end of a question is the sound of somebody talking to both of you.

¿Qué queréis? — 👂 what do you two want? — the shorter, blunter version of ¿qué vais a tomar?
Tenéis que esperar. — 👂 you two will have to wait — usually said with a nod at the queue

One sound note, for the two words you do say

Everything about Spanish vowels that part one drilled into you — five of them, pure, bright, and held to the last letter — stops being true the moment you say a Catalan word. Catalan reduces its unstressed vowels: the -es on the end of moltes gràcies is not the Spanish 'ehs', it is a soft, half-swallowed 'uhs', and sortida comes out closer to soor-TEE-duh than to the Spanish sor-TEE-dah. This is not a rule to learn, it is a warning not to apply the rule you have. Copy what you hear on the recording and let the ending fall away; the punch on the stressed syllable is the part that carries, and that half of the hand still works exactly as it did. Basque and Galician are kinder to you here — Galician vowels behave broadly like Spanish ones, and Basque, for all its strangeness on the page, is pronounced almost exactly as it is written once you know that x is 'sh' and z is a plain 's'.

Moltes gràcies. — MOHL-tuhs GRAH-see-uhs — the two endings go soft, and the punch stays on the first syllable of each word
Kaixo. — KAI-shoh — the x is 'sh'. Written as it sounds, once you know that one letter
