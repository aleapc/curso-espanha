The three rungs, and the two openers

Everything in this part hangs off one opening: '¿me puede…?' — literally 'can you, for me'. What follows it is the job, and it goes in exactly as the dictionary gives it: repetir, hablar más despacio, escribir, ayudar, llamar un taxi, enseñar. Where the thing being repeated is already obvious — a price, a street name, a platform — the little 'lo' slides in: '¿me lo puede escribir?', '¿me lo puede enseñar?'. Treat 'me lo puede' as one word and stop looking at it. The two openers that come before the ladder are 'hablo un poco de español', which asks for patience instead of ending the conversation, and '¿habla inglés?', which is asked second and never first.

¿Me puede repetir, por favor? — Can you say that again for me? — rung one
¿Me puede hablar más despacio, por favor? — Can you speak more slowly for me? — rung two
¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor? — Can you write it down for me? — rung three, and the one that ends the problem
¿Me puede ayudar? — Can you help me? — three words, fetches a human
¿Me puede llamar un taxi? — Can you call me a taxi?
¿Me lo puede enseñar en el mapa? — Can you show me on the map?

'Puede' or 'puedes' — and why we only gave you one

Spain runs on 'tú' far more than the Spanish taught to Americans suggests: bars, shops, taxis, anyone your own age or younger. Save 'usted' for three places — police and officials, someone clearly elderly, and a formal counter. The catch is that the repair kit gets used precisely at those counters, and under pressure, which is why we gave you the 'usted' shape and left it there: '¿me puede…?' is correct with absolutely everybody, and nobody in Spain has ever been offended by it. If the person is young and relaxed you will hear '¿me puedes…?' come back at you, and it means the same thing. Do not stop to convert it.

Perdone / Perdona — Excuse me — usted / tú
¿Me puede…? / ¿Me puedes…? — Can you, for me…? — usted / tú
¿Cómo dice? / ¿Cómo dices? — Sorry, what? — usted / tú

When words fail: use the artefact

The most reliable communication tool you own is not a sentence, it is your phone screen and a pen. Show the address rather than pronouncing it. Show the booking rather than describing it. Have the price written down rather than decoding a number said fast. Have the pharmacist write the name of the medicine. Each of those moves the risk off your pronunciation and onto paper, and paper never mishears anything. A machine translator belongs here too, and there is no shame in it: type it, turn the screen round, and let them read. The sentence that unlocks the whole category is the third rung — '¿me lo puede escribir, por favor?'.

¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor? — Can you write it down for me, please?
¿Me lo puede enseñar en el mapa? — Can you show me on the map?
En un papel, por favor. — On paper, please. — the answer when they offer your phone instead

Why the padding costs you the answer

A request in Spain is short, and the courtesy lives in your tone and in one 'por favor' at the end rather than in five layers of 'would you mind'. Translate an English run-up literally and two things happen: the listener loses the thread of what you actually want, and the extra seconds are read as distance rather than as manners. This is the trap that hits the British traveller hardest, and it is not a rule about being rude — it is a rule about being heard. Drop the run-up, raise the volume a notch above what feels polite at home, and go in without waiting for a gap. The gap is not scheduled. Where this reverses: a doctor, a bank counter and anyone in uniform, where turns are taken properly and the longer form costs you nothing.
