Three railways, three tickets, and the barrier that traps you

Metro = the urban underground, run by the city, with its own fare. Cercanías (Rodalies in Catalonia) = Renfe's commuter rail, lines numbered C-1, C-2 and so on: a separate network with a separate ticket, and normally the one that serves the airport and the towns nearby. Media Distancia / AVE = long distance: reserved seat, luggage through an X-ray, gate closed before departure. The classic failure is buying a metro ticket, walking to a Cercanías barrier, and discovering that the two systems have never once shared a fare. City by city, 2026 figures, verify before you fly. MADRID: the paper single no longer exists; you tap a contactless bank card or phone at the barrier (accepted since 1 June 2026) or buy the red 'Tarjeta Transporte Público Multi', about €2.50, non-personal, rechargeable, ten-year validity — non-personal means the card carries no name, not that it carries two passengers: one card admits one traveller at a time. Airport supplement €3 to enter or leave the airport stations on a single or ten-trip ticket. BARCELONA: the T-casual is about €13 for ten journeys and is valid for ONE traveller only — it is not a card two of you take turns with, and it does NOT cover the airport; the airport by metro L9 Sud is its own fare zone at roughly €5.90 each way. Contactless on TMB is rolling out through 2026 at about €2.90 a trip with transfers inside 90 minutes. The rule with no table in it: more than three rides a day, buy a T-casual each and you are paying about €1.30 a journey instead of €2.90; airport by metro, budget the supplement separately, each way.

¿Esto es Cercanías o metro? — thehr-kah-NEE-ahs oh MEH-troh — Is this Cercanías or the metro?
¿Me puede recargar la tarjeta? — rreh-kar-GAR — Could you top the card up for me?

Validating: the machine nobody makes you use

On Cercanías and regional services you very often have to stamp a paper ticket at a small machine before the platform, and an unvalidated ticket is treated as no ticket at all. There is usually no gate forcing you to do it, and no member of staff watching — the check happens on board. On the Madrid metro with contactless, or on a long-distance train with a seat number, there is nothing to stamp: the barrier or the guard is the whole check. Two words to recognise on the yellow box: VALIDAR (stamp) and RECARGA (top up). If you cannot see a machine and cannot see a gate, ask — '¿Tengo que validar?' is six words and costs nothing, and nobody has ever been told off for stamping a ticket that did not need it.

¿Tengo que validar el billete? — bah-lee-DAR — Do I have to validate the ticket?
¿Tengo que hacer transbordo? — trans-BOR-doh — Do I have to change?

Cercanías is not free any more (the 2026 correction)

The recurring free-travel schemes on Cercanías ended on 31 December 2025 and were replaced by the unified 2026 arrangement. Only the Asturias and Cantabria networks remain free through 31 December 2026, and even then subject to a minimum number of journeys per four-month period. The €10 monthly youth pass (for people born between 2000 and 2011) requires Spanish nationality or residency and is therefore not available to a visitor. Any blog, video or guide from 2023–2024 telling you Spanish commuter rail is free was accurate at the time and is wrong now — which is worth knowing not because free trains matter much to a fortnight's holiday, but because it tells you how old the rest of that guide is.

Un billete de Cercanías a Aranjuez, por favor. — thehr-kah-NEE-ahs — A Cercanías ticket to Aranjuez, please.
¿Este billete vale para el aeropuerto? — — Is this ticket valid for the airport?

The pickpocket map, and why it is a relief rather than a warning

What happens to visitors on Spanish public transport is 'hurto' — theft by stealth and distraction — and not 'robo con violencia', robbery with force. That distinction is not a comforting technicality: it dictates the defence. Against force you would need luck; against stealth you need attention and body position, and both are free. The map is unusually specific in Barcelona: the cases concentrate on metro lines L1, L3 and L5, the stations named most often are Liceu and Sagrada Família (both L3, the tourist line), and the hours are rush hours in daylight — peaks 08:00–09:30 and 17:00–20:00. Three body rules cover most of it: backpack round to the front on the metro, phone never on a terrace table, bag never on the back of a chair. And the direction of travel is good: the joint Mossos / Guàrdia Urbana / TMB operation 'Pla Kanpai', extended to buses in January 2026, cut theft on public transport by 40% in the first half of 2026.

¿Dónde está la salida? — — Where's the way out? (the sentence you want at the top of the escalator, not at the bottom)

Why there is a scanner, and what 'thirty minutes' actually buys you

Spanish high-speed stations run X-ray machines and metal detectors — Spain is the only Western country that still does this for high-speed rail, at a reported cost of around €13 million a year and roughly 360 security staff, and the International Union of Railways advises against it. It is a live argument, not a settled policy: the president of Renfe has publicly raised scrapping it, the government has defended keeping it, and the scanner has already been removed on the Atlantic Axis while remaining for Avant, AVE and Avlo. For your trip, assume it is there. Renfe's own recommendation is 30 minutes before departure, with the stated aim of closing the access gate 5 minutes before the train goes. The mental model to install is 'small airport', not 'British train' — because the failure mode is not a delay, it is a missed train with no late boarding available. Luggage, Renfe policy current in 2026: up to 3 items per passenger, totalling no more than 25 kg and 290 cm of summed dimensions, largest single item not exceeding 85 × 55 × 35 cm, enforced with physical gauges at the access points. Electric scooters and unicycles are banned outright on fire-risk grounds. No fee, no check-in, no tagging. The word is 'maleta' in Spain ('valija' is Argentine); left luggage, where a station has it, is 'consigna'.

¿A qué hora cierra el control de acceso? — — What time does the access gate close?
¿Hay consigna en la estación? — kon-SEEG-nah — Is there left luggage in the station?

Three operators, one set of tracks, and the 60–90 day window

Iryo's arrival in 2022 broke thirty years of Renfe monopoly and competition pulled prices down hard on the competitive routes; Ouigo is the low-cost operator. Renfe and Iryo typically release their cheapest fares 60 to 90 days ahead, and Ouigo opens sales as much as 9 months out. Prices then behave like airline fares — last-minute can be 2 to 3 times the cost, and on busy routes there may be no seat at all. This is why buying the train belongs on the pre-departure checklist alongside your eSIM: it is not a thing you can fix at the station. Check all three operators for the same journey; they run the same lines. And fares, security rules and luggage limits all move — check the operator's own page before you fly.

¿Hay billetes para el tren de las once? — OHN-theh — Are there tickets for the eleven o'clock train?
¿Este billete se puede cambiar? — — Is this ticket changeable?

vía / andén, and the false friend that costs you the train

Renfe says 'vía' (track); the metro says 'andén' (platform). They mean the same thing to you and only one of them appears on any given board. When there is still nothing next to your train, '¿De qué vía sale?' is worth asking rather than standing and staring. And the false friend to burn in: 'once' is ELEVEN, not 'once'; one single time is 'una vez'. It bites hardest on train times, restaurant bookings and tour departures. Both 'once' and 'doce' carry the Spanish 'th' — 'OHN-theh', 'DOH-theh' — which is also how you tell 'cincuenta' (50) from 'sesenta' (60) when somebody reads you a price. The numbers in this part are all on the receiving side: you read them off a board, or you hear them from a guard. You are never asked to produce one.

El tren de las once, vía doce. — — The eleven o'clock train, track twelve.
¿Este tren para en Córdoba? — — Does this train stop at Córdoba? (the AVE often doesn't; Media Distancia does)

You missed it: the protocol

Do not queue at the ticket machines. Go straight to 'Atención al Cliente', and go immediately — the further you get from your scheduled departure, the fewer options exist. Say '¿Puedo cambiar el billete?' and stay calm and seated in tone. What happens next depends on your fare: the cheapest tickets are frequently non-changeable and you may have to buy again, but travellers who present themselves promptly and politely are routinely put on the next departure. This is a protocol, not a magic sentence — the sentence just opens the conversation, and '¿Hay otro tren esta tarde?' is the one that saves the day whichever way the first answer goes.

¿Puedo cambiar el billete? — — Can I change the ticket?
¿Hay otro tren esta tarde? — — Is there another train this afternoon?
