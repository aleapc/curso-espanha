Why the town comes before the country

Spain is a state of seventeen autonomous communities plus two cities on the North African coast, and the arrangement is not decorative: health, education, policing in some places, and the language of the street in several of them are run at that level and not from Madrid. Add to that a country where a very large number of people can still point at the village their family came from, and you get an ordinary conversational habit: the answer to 'where are you from' is normally the smallest unit the other person is likely to place, not the largest. Nothing about this is nationalism, and nobody is making a point when they do it — it is closer to the way somebody in Britain answers with the town rather than 'England'. What is different is how far down it goes and how much conversation sits on top of it: the food changes, the festival changes, the word for the same sandwich changes, and all of that is available to you the moment you ask '¿de qué parte?'. It is also why the question does more work than any compliment you could pay. A compliment ends; a question about somebody's town is a door.

¿De qué parte? — deh keh PAR-teh — which part? The three words that get you the village
¿Y allí qué se come? — ee ah-YEE keh seh KOH-meh — and what do people eat there? The natural second question

The inheritance: an empire, and then a long time of being the poor south

You do not need any of this at a table, and none of it goes in your mouth — but it explains the two reactions in this part better than anything else. Spain ran one of the largest empires in history and then spent a long stretch of the modern period as the poorer, sunnier end of Western Europe: outside the industrial core, under a dictatorship until the mid-1970s, joining the European Community only in 1986, and then acquiring, in a single generation, motorways, high-speed rail and a middle class. That sequence leaves a very specific sensitivity, and it is not pride in the abstract. It is a low tolerance for being handled as the picturesque south — cheap, hot, late, a bit chaotic, lovely people. Every one of those adjectives is one a visitor deploys as a compliment. Read in that light, 'it's so cheap here' and 'is it always this hot?' stop being random irritants and become the same sentence twice. The counter-move is not to avoid the subjects; it is to talk about the specific place in front of you rather than about the country as a category.

Estaba buenísimo, de verdad. — deh behr-DAHDH — it was genuinely delicious. The compliment that lands on the person, not on the price
¡Qué ciudad más bonita! — keh thyoo-DHAHDH mahs boh-NEE-tah — what a lovely city. About this one, not about the country

The licence to complain, and why it is not yours

Complaining about the country to a foreigner is common enough to be a genre: the paperwork, the politicians, the trains that only go to Madrid, the salaries, the summer. It is often funny, it is frequently affectionate, and it is almost never an invitation. The rule of thumb that works everywhere, not only here: the licence to run a house down belongs to the people who live in it. What the guest is offered is the laugh, not the second signature. If you want a test you can apply at speed, listen for whether the sentence ends in a full stop or in a look. 'Aquí no funciona nada' with a shrug is a bit of theatre and wants a laugh. '¿Y a ti qué te parece esto?' — asked straight, eyes up — is a real question, and there a small, specific, honest answer is exactly right, including a critical one. The failure mode is the visitor who mistakes the first for the second and starts listing. That is where the temperature drops, and it drops without anybody saying anything, which is why so many people never learn they did it.

Aquí no funciona nada, ya lo verás. — 👂 nothing here works, you'll see — theatre, wants a laugh, not a verdict
Pues a mí me encantó el mercado. — pwehs ah MEE meh en-kan-TOH — well, I loved the market. One named thing, and the list stops

Italy, Mexico, and the Spanish you were taught

Two comparisons come up constantly from visitors and both shorten the conversation, for the same reason and to different degrees. Italy is the neighbour: same sea, same tomatoes, same rice-and-saffron rumour, and a football-shaped rivalry that is entirely alive between themselves. Coming from a foreigner, 'this is like Italian food' is heard as being told your kitchen is a regional variant of somebody else's. Mexico is further and lands harder, because it is not a comparison at all, it is a substitution: tacos, mariachi and the wide-brimmed hat are another country on the other side of an ocean, with its own history with this one, and the visitor who arrives expecting them has effectively skipped Spain. The linguistic version is quieter and just as common: the app most people start with is largely Latin American, as is a good deal of the Spanish taught in schools in the States, so the accent you turn up with may have come from thousands of miles the other side of this country — it depends entirely on where you got it. Nobody minds and nobody will correct you — but it is worth knowing that 'the Spanish I learned sounds nothing like yours' is, from where he is standing, a slightly odd thing to announce. The reliable way out of all three is the same and it is one sentence: ask what is from here.

¿Hay algo típico de aquí? — ai AL-goh TEE-pee-koh deh ah-KEE — is there something typical of here? The question that replaces the comparison
¿Esto es de aquí? — EHS-toh ehs deh ah-KEE — is this from here? The one-second version, said pointing at a plate

Guiri, and the one joke that is yours

'Guiri' is what a northern European tourist gets called here. It is slang, it is usually not hostile, and it is often affectionate — the barman who uses it about you across the room is not insulting you, he is placing you, and quite often he is doing it with some warmth. There is exactly one joke about it that works in a visitor's mouth, and it is the joke about yourself: 'soy el guiri, ya lo sé' — I'm the guiri, I know. It works because it does two things at once: it shows you understood the word, and it hands the teasing back without taking it seriously. What does not work is using it about somebody else, or reaching for the affectionate insults you have just heard two friends throw at each other. That register is covered in the part before this one, and it stays receptive for a reason: mock insults between friends run on years of history you do not have yet. Yours is the self-deprecating one, and it is enough. Worth adding, because it comes up in the same breath: 'guiri' is about how you are dressed and how you are behaving as much as where you are from, and there is a version of it that costs money — shirtless two streets from the beach is a fine in several cities, and that is in the afternoon part.

Soy el guiri, ya lo sé. — soy el GEE-ree, yah loh SEH — I'm the guiri, I know. The one joke about yourself
¡Este ya es de la casa! — 👂 this one's one of ours now — what you get back for making it
