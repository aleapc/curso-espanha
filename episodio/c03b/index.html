Spain is four languages and one very old argument

You don't need to speak Catalan, Basque or Galician — you need to recognise them, and to know which subject to leave alone

Episode 3 · 3B 1 / 23
Guide

Part 3B, and let's start with the fact most visitors arrive without: Spain has four languages. Castilian Spanish — the one you're learning — is official everywhere, and it is the language you will be answered in the moment anyone hears your accent. Alongside it, Catalan is co-official in Catalonia, Valencia and the Balearics; Basque in the Basque Country and part of Navarre; Galician in Galicia. Roughly speaking, that's about two in every five Spaniards living somewhere with a second official language. Now the permission, and it's a big one, so I'll say it before anything else: you will not offend anybody by speaking Spanish in Barcelona. Not a soul. Bon dia is a courtesy, never a requirement, and courtesy is free. What you do need is to recognise the other three when they turn up on a door, a menu or a departure board — because they will. Who speaks what, and where

Castilian Spanish is official across the whole territory and is what this course teaches. Catalan is co-official in Catalonia, the Balearics and the Valencian Community (where it is officially called valencià); Basque (euskara) in the Basque Country and northern Navarre; Galician (galego) in Galicia. Catalan and Galician are Romance languages — you will half-recognise a lot of written Catalan and most written Galician. Basque is unrelated to any known living language, which is why nothing in it looks familiar. You will be served in Spanish everywhere; the co-official language matters to you almost entirely in writing.

Bon dia · Bona tarda · Si us plau · Gràcies · Adéu — Catalan: good morning · good afternoon · please · thanks · bye
Kaixo · Eskerrik asko / Bo día · Grazas — Basque: hello · thank you / Galician: good morning · thanks

The sign module (recognition only)

Catalan: obert / tancat (open / closed) · sortida (exit) · entrada · lavabos or serveis (toilets) · estirar / empènyer (pull / push — note that estirar is PULL, which trips up anyone who has just learned Spanish tirar) · esmorzar / dinar / sopar (breakfast / lunch / dinner, and useful for reading opening hours) · preu (price) · pa (bread) · aigua (water). Basque: irteera (exit) · komuna (toilet) · itxita (closed). Galician: saída (exit) · aberto / pechado (open / closed). Nothing here is ever produced out loud — it goes in your eyes, not your mouth.

The political topic: no legal risk, all social risk

Unlike some destinations, there is no legal exposure here at all — Spain debates Catalonia, the monarchy and the Civil War openly and constantly. What you can lose is the evening. Don't open the subject with strangers. If it opens on you, ask and listen: 'No conozco bien el tema, cuénteme' (usted) or 'cuéntame' (tú) hands the floor back and almost always ends the pressure. Never infer someone's position from their city, accent or language — Catalan-speaking Catalans have polled majority-against independence in multiple measurements. Avoid both 'Spain and Catalonia' as separate countries and 'it's all just Spain', which offend opposite sides. Football is not neutral ground; claiming to support both Barça and Madrid is worse than picking one.

The 2024–25 protests, read correctly

In July 2024 protesters in Barcelona sprayed water pistols at tourists on terraces; in June 2025 coordinated protests took place in Barcelona, Palma, Lisbon, Venice and Naples under the SET network, with 'tourist go home' stickers on hotel doors. The declared root is housing: local authorities have cited a rise of about 68% in Barcelona housing costs over the decade. It targets the model, not the individual. Two behaviours put a visitor on the wrong side of it instantly and both are free to avoid: wheeling a suitcase through a residential street at 2am, and noise on a shared stairwell. 'Guiri' is slang for a northern European tourist and is normally affectionate or neutral, not a slur.
