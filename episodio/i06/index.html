Why you are given three finished sentences instead of a machine

Spanish has a full apparatus for talking about what happened yesterday, and learning it properly takes months that you do not have before this flight. What a fortnight in Spain actually asks of you is much smaller: somebody wants to know where you went, what you ate and whether you liked it. So we have handed you the three finished sentences that answer exactly that, learnt whole, with one slot each — the place, the plate, the thing. 'Fui', 'comí' and 'encantó' are not being taught to you as forms with rules attached; they are being handed over as the front halves of three sentences, the way 'la cuenta' was handed over as one word. There is deliberately no fourth. Everything else that happened to you yesterday either fits into one of these three or gets pointed at, and pointing is a legitimate half of this part rather than a failure of it. If you find yourself wanting to say 'we were going to go but then it rained', the honest answer is that the sentence is not worth the fortnight it would cost you — say 'ayer fui a' somewhere else instead, and the evening carries on.

Ayer fui a Toledo. Comí cocido. Me encantó. — ah-YEHR fwee ah toh-LEH-dhoh — the three run together as one answer in about four seconds, and the third one can drop its ending when you have only just named the thing
Hoy no he hecho nada. — oy noh eh EH-choh NAH-dhah — 👂 'I've done nothing today' — you will hear this back from them constantly, and it is a complete and respectable answer

The little word that comes with the place

'Ayer fui a' takes a place name straight — a Toledo, a Sevilla, a Bilbao — and that is the easy half. When the place has a 'the' in front of it in English, Spanish keeps its own version of that 'the' and glues it on: a la playa, a la catedral, a la plaza. And when the word is a masculine one, the two little words collapse into one syllable: a + el becomes al. Al mercado. Al museo. Al parque. This is not something to work out at the counter. Learn the place with its little word already stuck on, exactly as you learned 'la cuenta' and 'el baño' rather than 'cuenta' and 'baño', and it will never come up again. Get it the wrong way round and nothing whatsoever happens: 'ayer fui a el museo' is understood instantly and read as an accent, not an error. The only reason we recorded all three shapes is so that your ear has heard each of them before your mouth needs one.

Ayer fui al pueblo de al lado. — ah-YEHR fwee al PWEH-bloh deh al LAH-dhoh — yesterday I went to the next village along
Ayer fui a la sierra. — ah-YEHR fwee ah lah SYEH-rrah — yesterday I went up into the hills

'Me encantó' — and why the end of it never moves

'Me encantó' is the one that surprises people, because the thing you loved sits after it and yet the front does not change to match: me encantó el mercado, me encantó la plaza, me encantó Toledo. Strictly speaking the sentence is built the other way round from the English — the market is doing the delighting and you are the one it happens to — which is the same shape as 'me gusta' from the last part. That is genuinely all there is to know, and knowing it buys you nothing at a bar table, so treat it as a gift: one front, anything you like on the end, no agreement to think about. The one place it does move is when you loved several things — 'me encantaron las plazas' — and we have deliberately not taught you that, because naming one thing is the whole point of the sentence and naming three is what makes the other person stop listening. If you want more force, 'me encantó, de verdad' does it with two extra words, and those two turn up properly in the next part.

Me encantó el barrio. — meh en-kan-TOH el BAH-rryoh — I loved the neighbourhood
Me encantó, de verdad. — meh en-kan-TOH, deh behr-DAHDH — I really did love it (the 'de verdad' is the same one from the thank-you)

'Estuvo genial', 'todavía no', and being asked as a pair

'Estuvo genial' is two words about how a thing went — an evening, a concert, a day out — and it needs nothing after it. Its neighbours are 'estuvo muy bien' (it was very good) and 'estuvo bien' (it was fine, and it is genuinely fine). You will also hear 'ha estado genial' from people talking about something that has only just finished; both are correct, nobody is counting, and one of them is enough. 'Todavía no' is the other two-word whole, and it is doing more work than it looks: asked whether you have been to somewhere, 'no' closes the subject and 'not yet' asks to be told about it, which is exactly the reply that gets you the directions. Its partner is 'ya' — already, been, done — and between them they answer almost every 'have you…?' you will be asked in a fortnight. The other thing worth knowing before you get asked: when you are travelling as a pair, the question will very often arrive in the plural — '¿y qué habéis hecho?', '¿os ha gustado?' — because they are addressing both of you at once. You answer in the singular and nothing at all goes wrong: 'ayer fui a la playa' is a perfectly ordinary reply to a question aimed at two people, because you are answering for yourself. If you would like the pair version, it is one word learnt whole — 'fuimos' instead of 'fui' — and it is entirely optional. Recognising 'habéis' and 'os' as 'you two' is the part that actually matters, and that lives in your ear rather than your mouth.

Estuvo muy bien. — ehs-TOO-boh mwee byen — it was very good
Todavía no. ¿Merece la pena? — toh-dhah-BEE-ah noh. meh-REH-theh lah PEH-nah — not yet. Is it worth it? (the second half is optional, and it hands the turn straight back)
¿Y qué habéis hecho hoy? — ee keh ah-BEHS EH-choh oy — 👂 'and what have you two done today?' — answer it in the singular

What the three sentences are actually buying you

The reason this part exists is not conversation practice. Up to now everything the course has given you is something you use to ask for things — a drink, a table, a platform, a chemist. These three are the first ones that hand something back, and that changes what the other person does next. A bar that has served you twice and knows you went to Toledo yesterday will tell you where to go tomorrow; a bar that has served you twice and got a smile and a nod will keep serving you and tell you nothing, which is exactly what happens to almost everybody. It is worth being blunt about the size of the difference, because it is the whole reason to bother: the recommendation you get across a counter is the one that is not in the guidebook, is not on the square, and is not full. And the mechanics of collecting it are already in your hands — you say the three sentences, they tell you where to go, and when the name goes past too fast you ask them to write it down, which is the oldest sentence in this course.

¿Y usted dónde va a comer? — ee oos-TEHDH DOHN-deh bah ah koh-MEHR — 👂 'and where do you eat?' — the question you will hear a local ask another local; you do not need to produce it, but recognising it tells you what a real recommendation sounds like
¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor? — meh loh PWEH-dheh ehs-kree-BEER — could you write it down for me, please? (part three, doing the most valuable work it will ever do)
