Estaba, not está — and why the -ísimo does the work

About a plate you have finished, the natural thing to say is 'estaba buenísimo': the food was good while it lasted, and it has now stopped existing. 'Está buenísimo' is what you say with a fork still in it. Both are correct and nobody will blink at either, so this is not a rule to hold in your head at the table — it is simply why the recorded version says estaba. The ending -ísimo is a whole extra layer of enthusiasm bolted onto the adjective: bueno becomes buenísimo, rico becomes riquísimo (the c hardens to a qu so the sound stays the same). It is very common, it is not childish or over the top, and it is the single cheapest way to sound like you meant it rather than like you are being polite. One warning worth having: 'soy bueno' about yourself does not mean you are a good person, and 'estoy buena' about yourself is a comment on your looks. The food gets buenísimo; leave your own adjectives alone.

Estaba todo buenísimo. — ehs-TAH-bah TOH-dhoh bweh-NEE-see-moh — everything was delicious (the whole-table version, at the end of a meal)
Está riquísimo. — ehs-TAH rree-KEE-see-moh — this is delicious (with the fork still in your hand)

¡Qué + the thing + más + the word! — one shape, four uses

The second half of the frame is even shorter: ¡Qué rico! ¡Qué bueno! ¡Qué amable! Two words and three beats, which is why it survives a mouthful of tortilla. When you want to say it about a thing rather than about the taste, the thing goes in the middle and 'más' props it up: ¡Qué sitio más bonito! ¡Qué ciudad más bonita! ¡Qué plaza más bonita! The ending of the last word follows the thing in the middle — bonito for sitio, bonita for ciudad — and this is the one place where you take it by ear and refuse to think about it. Getting it the wrong way round costs you precisely nothing: it is heard as an accent, not as an error, and the compliment arrives intact. You can also drop 'más' entirely (¡Qué sitio bonito!) and it still works. Two more that come free: '¡Qué buena pinta!' is said to a plate as it lands — la pinta is the look of a thing — and '¡Qué rico está esto!' is the long version of the short one, for when you have time.

¡Qué plaza más bonita! — keh PLAH-thah mahs boh-NEE-tah — what a lovely square
¡Qué amable, muchas gracias! — keh ah-MAH-bleh — how kind, thank you very much (for a person, never for food)

The thank you that gets heard

British courtesy spends a please and a thank you on every turn of an exchange, and in Spain that count reads as ceremony — not rude, but formal, and formality at a counter is distance. The local shape is one, at the end, meant: 'muchas gracias' with your eyes up, and 'de verdad' on the end when the person actually did something for you. 'Muy amable' — you're very kind — is the other one, and it is the natural reply when somebody has gone out of their way. The exception runs the other way and it matters: when somebody has genuinely put themselves out, walked you three streets, rung a taxi, opened up after closing, then thank them properly and twice, and nothing about that is too much. What you are trading is quantity for weight. Bank one more, for the room rather than the person: 'que aproveche' is what you say to people who are eating as you pass them, and it is said between strangers all day long. Waiters say it when the food lands; you say it on your way out.

Muchas gracias, de verdad. — deh behr-DAHDH — thank you, really. The two words on the end are the whole point
Que aproveche. — keh ah-proh-BEH-cheh — enjoy your meal, said in passing to anybody eating

The glass, and what not to make of it

'¡Salud!' is the toast, it is one word, and it covers every table you will sit at. Glasses go up, you say it, you drink. There is no speech, no clinking ritual you have to get right, and no round-the-table order to learn. If somebody says something longer and you did not catch it, '¡Salud!' back is a complete and correct answer. Two neighbours of it that you will hear and never need to produce: 'que vaya bien' is said to somebody leaving, and 'que aproveche' is the one for eating. What we are deliberately not doing here is turning a toast into a performance: the whole social weight of it is in raising the glass and looking up, and the word is just the noise that goes with it. The evening at somebody's home — what to take, where to sit, when to leave, and how to accept the invitation in the first place — is a part of its own further on.

¡Salud! — sah-LOODH — cheers. One word, every table, every time
Que vaya bien. — keh BAH-yah byen — 👂 all the best, said to somebody heading off

Sending something back without it becoming an event

The sentence that works is a flat statement of fact with nobody in it: 'Perdone, esto está frío.' There is no accusation, no story about how long you waited and no apology in front of it, which is exactly why it gets a quick fix rather than a defensive one. Other endings for the same shape: 'esto no es lo que he pedido' (this is not what I ordered), 'esto está crudo' (this is underdone), 'esta copa está sucia' (this glass is dirty). Then the ask, which is an opener you already own: '¿Me lo puede cambiar?' — the me lo travels on the front of it as if it were part of the word, and you never take it apart. Volume and posture do more than vocabulary here: stay sitting, keep the voice you order in, and let the fact stand on its own. A tourist performing outrage is theatre and is treated as theatre; a customer stating a fact is work, and work gets done. If it is a line on the bill rather than a plate, the ladder from part fourteen takes over — the itemised bill first, then naming the charge, then the complaint form — and it is the same voice all the way up. And the one thing that never gets this easy register is an allergy: that is the flat, serious sentence from part thirteen, said looking at them, with the card in your hand.

Perdone, esto no es lo que he pedido. — noh es loh keh eh peh-DEE-dhoh — sorry, this is not what I ordered
¿Me lo puede cambiar, por favor? — meh loh PWEH-dheh kam-BYAR — could you change it for me, please?

Tú, usted, and who you are talking to when you say it

The compliments in this part are the same words whoever you are talking to, which is half the reason they are the ones we chose: 'estaba buenísimo' and '¡qué rico!' carry no decision about the person opposite. Only two sentences here lean either way. '¿Le ha gustado el arroz?' is what you will hear from an older waiter or in a more formal dining room — le is the usted version — and you do not have to answer in kind; naming the dish is the answer. And '¿Me lo puede cambiar?' is the same shape you learned in part three for '¿me lo puede escribir?' — we recorded it that way so the opener you already know does not change on you halfway through the course, not because a restaurant demands it. In a young bar the tú version, '¿me lo puedes cambiar?', is flying about all evening, and if somebody uses tú with you, use it back. Nothing in this part breaks if you get it the other way round.

¿Te ha gustado? — teh ah goos-TAH-dhoh — 👂 the tú version of the same question, in a younger place
¿Me lo puedes cambiar? — meh loh PWEH-dhehs kam-BYAR — the tú version of the ask, for a bar where you are already on tú
