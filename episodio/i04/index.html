Why nine o'clock is early, and what that buys you

Typically, and none of this is official: the main meal of the day is at two, there is a snack around five or six, and dinner runs from nine, with kitchens taking their last orders somewhere around half eleven. Work backwards from that and the evening comes out at the shape you have been seeing — a square that is emptier at eight than at half ten, and a bar that has a queue at eleven. The practical consequence is not authenticity theatre, it is a filter: a place happily serving full dinners at seven is, almost by definition, a place living on people who do not know the hours, and that is also where the inflated bill and the price-free tourist menu live. There is one straightforward exception and it is worth writing on your hand: the transport does not move with the evening. The last metro, the last cercanías and the train home all run to a printed timetable, and the timetable does not know what kind of night you are having. Book dinner at half nine, and check the last train before you order the second bottle rather than after.

La cocina cierra a las once y media. — lah koh-THEE-nah THYEH-rrah — 👂 the kitchen shuts at half eleven
¿Hasta qué hora está abierto? — AHS-tah keh OH-rah ehs-TAH ah-BYEHR-toh — how late are you open?

The turn: invito yo, la próxima, and when to ask to split

Drinks are bought in whole turns rather than by the glass. One person says 'invito yo' — this one is on me — and the next round is somebody else's, and it carries on like that across the evening and often across several bars. Nobody is keeping a ledger; the assumption is simply that it evens out, and it usually does. Two things follow. First, if you sit there paying for your own drink each time, you are not being careful, you are quietly stepping outside the group, and it is noticed. Second, if somebody gets in before you, the repair is one sentence said immediately: 'la próxima la pago yo' — I'll get the next one — and then you actually do it. The variants all work the same way: 'esta la pago yo' for this one, 'te invito' when it is one other person, and 'invita la casa' is what you will hear when the bar itself is buying, which does happen. Where turns genuinely do not fit — eight people, half of them eating, or a table of strangers you will not see again — the sentence is '¿podemos pagar por separado?', and it has a clock on it: say it while you are ordering. Once the bill has been added up as one number, unpicking it is a job for the till and the answer is often no. 'A partes iguales' — equal shares — is the other ordinary way out, and it is far more common at a Spanish table than working out who had the extra beer.

Esta la pago yo. — EHS-tah lah PAH-goh yoh — this one is on me (the same move, different words)
Pagamos a partes iguales. — ah PAR-tehs ee-GWAH-lehs — let's split it equally

Getting up from a table that is still going

Leaving is a two-move business and the first move is not expected to work. You stand, you say 'yo me voy yendo' — literally 'I am going going', which is exactly the softness it carries — and what comes back is '¡pero si es pronto!' or 'anda, quédate un poco más'. That is not somebody trying to trap you; it is the ordinary shape of the exchange, and going straight out of the door on the first attempt reads as if something has gone wrong. The second move is where you actually leave, and it wants a reason attached: 'es que mañana madrugo' — I have an early start tomorrow — which is the one everybody uses because it cannot be argued with. Then 'hasta luego' or 'nos vemos' and you go, and crucially the table does not go with you. Nobody stands up, nobody puts a coat on, nobody feels obliged to call it a night because you have. Two you will hear on the way out and never need to say: 'que sigáis bien' and 'que os vaya bien' are what stays behind wishes the leaver. If you want the shorter version of everything above: say it once, take the protest, say it again with a reason, and walk.

Anda, quédate un poco más. — AHN-dah, KEH-dhah-teh oon POH-koh mahs — 👂 go on, stay a bit longer
Nos vemos mañana. — nohs BEH-mohs mah-NYAH-nah — see you tomorrow

The line on the pavement, with the numbers and the dates on them

The alcohol is not the offence; the ground is. Drinking on a public street is a minor offence under national law — Ley Orgánica 4/2015, article 37.17 — at €100 to €600, and that is the national floor rather than the whole story. Barcelona's civility ordinance, approved in December 2025 and in force since February 2026, takes it to €1,500 where children are around or in a night-time noise zone, and prices organising or promoting a pub crawl at €3,000. In the Balearic excess-tourism zones — Playa de Palma, Magaluf, Sant Antoni — drinking outside an authorised area is €500 to €1,500, happy hours and organised pub crawls are banned outright, and shops cannot sell you alcohol between 21:30 and 08:00. Two metres away, at a licensed terrace table on the same kerb, the identical drink is simply a drink. The change that killed the old folk wisdom is worth knowing: the same Barcelona ordinance obliges non-residents to pay on the spot and the Guàrdia Urbana carries card terminals, because collection used to be near zero once the tourist had flown home. So the two questions that belong in your mouth are '¿de cuánto es la multa?' and '¿me lo puede dar por escrito?' — the receipt is your only proof you paid. Amounts and ordinances move: these are as of 2026, and a municipal rule can change in a single council meeting.

¿De cuánto es la multa? — deh KWAN-toh es lah MOOL-tah — how much is it?
¿Me lo puede dar por escrito, por favor? — meh loh PWEH-dheh dar por ehs-KREE-toh — could you give me that in writing?

The flat, the stairwell, and why the host panics

Quiet hours — 'las horas de descanso' — typically run from ten or eleven at night to eight in the morning, and they are set by the town hall and by the building's own rules rather than nationally. There is no national fines table, so treat any single figure you read online with suspicion; what is reliable is where the complaint goes. The neighbour rings the local police, not your host, and in a licensed tourist flat repeated complaints put the flat's licence at risk. That, and not his sleep, is what is behind the message at half past midnight from somebody who was charm itself at check-in. The body actions cost nothing: library voice on the stairwell after midnight, no inner courtyard or terrace after eleven, and no suitcase wheels down a residential street at two in the morning — that last one is also, in a city arguing about housing, the single most reliable way to make an enemy in four seconds. If somebody does knock, do not argue through the door: 'perdón, bajamos la voz ahora mismo' ends it, because regret plus immediate action is the pairing that defuses almost anything here. And note the inversion that catches British visitors out: the bar itself is loud, nobody minds, and lowering your voice in there is the only thing that marks you out. Volume belongs in the bar. Quiet belongs on the stairs.

¿A qué hora son las horas de descanso? — lahs OH-rahs deh dehs-KAN-soh — what are the quiet hours?
Perdón, bajamos la voz ahora mismo. — bah-KHAH-mohs lah bohth — sorry, we'll keep it down right now

Otra, and the two-way question — where else they go

'Otra' on its own is a complete sentence when the empty glass says the rest, and everything you hang off it travels with it: 'otra ronda' for the table, 'otra caña', 'otro vino', 'otra de esas' pointing at the tray. The ending of the word follows the thing hanging off it — otra ronda, otro vino — and you take that by ear and refuse to think about it, because getting it the other way round is heard as an accent and not as a mistake. The two-way question from part twenty-one does the same trick in a different direction: instead of asking what is good, which produces an answer you then have to understand, you offer two things and let them pick one, which produces an answer you already know. '¿Cuál me recomienda, el tinto o el blanco?' at a wine bar; '¿cuál está mejor, este o aquel?' pointing at two trays; '¿cuál cierra más tarde?' when the choice is between two bars rather than two drinks. It works because you have made the answer binary, and a binary answer survives a loud room, a strong accent and a fortnight of jet lag. Word to bank while we are here: 'la última' is what the table calls the last one of the night, and it is almost never the last one.

Otra de esas, por favor. — OH-trah deh EH-sahs — another one of those, please (pointing works)
¿Nos tomamos la última? — lah OOL-tee-mah — 👂 shall we have the last one? (it is not the last one)
