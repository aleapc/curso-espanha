What you can buy without a prescription — and what you can't

Over the counter in Spain: paracetamol and ibuprofen in standard doses, anti-inflammatories, antihistamines, decongestants, antacids and stomach protectors, anti-diarrhoeals, creams, dressings, sun cream. Prescription only: antibiotics, with no exceptions — a pharmacy or website offering antibiotics without a prescription is breaking the law — and the higher-strength painkillers you may be used to buying freely at home. The packet of anything sold freely carries the words 'sin receta médica'. If you take something regularly, bring it with you rather than assuming you can replace it at the counter, and bring the box so the pharmacist can read the active ingredient.

¿Me puede recomendar algo para el dolor de garganta? — Something for a sore throat?
¿Esto necesita receta? — Does this need a prescription?

GHIC treats you. Insurance brings you home.

For British travellers, the UK GHIC gives access to medically necessary state healthcare in Spain on the same terms as a Spanish resident — often free or very cheap — and it covers emergencies and sudden illness regardless of pre-existing conditions. It does not cover repatriation, private hospital treatment, or anything non-medical. The UK government itself says the card is not a substitute for travel insurance. For American travellers there is no reciprocal healthcare agreement with Spain at all, US health plans typically don't pay for treatment there, and many hospitals ask non-residents to pay up front — which makes travel insurance a prerequisite rather than a nice idea. Either way, the question that protects you is the same: '¿Esto es público o privado?', asked before treatment, not after. And ask for 'la factura' — an itemised invoice — or your insurer has nothing to reimburse.

¿Dónde está el centro de salud? — Where's the (public) health centre?
¿Me puede dar la factura para el seguro? — Can I have the invoice for my insurance?

The invisible queue

In markets, bakeries, fishmongers, butchers, ticket windows and health centre waiting rooms, Spaniards don't form a line — they form a memory. You arrive, you ask the room '¿quién es el último?', one person acknowledges, and from then on you go after that person and the next arrival goes after you. It's efficient, it lets people wander off to another counter, and it is completely invisible to anyone who doesn't ask. If someone asks you the question later, just say 'yo' — me.

¿Quién es el último? — Who's last?
Yo. / Yo soy el último. — Me. / I'm last. — the answer when someone asks you

Heat is a medical item, not a weather item

Spanish summers have been breaking records, with national heat alerts and hundreds of excess deaths in a single hot month. For a visitor from a country where thirty degrees is front-page news, this changes behaviour rather than clothing: the two-to-six window in the interior and the south is not walking time, air-conditioned transport stops being a luxury, and a twenty-five-minute walk suggested by a map at three in the afternoon in Seville in August is bad advice. Recognise the phrase 'ola de calor' — heatwave — and the AEMET colour alerts: yellow, orange, red. And note that this is precisely why the Spanish day is shaped the way it is: the long lunch and the late dinner are not laziness, they're physics.

Un agua bien fría, por favor. — A really cold water, please.
¿Hay aire acondicionado? — Is there air conditioning?
